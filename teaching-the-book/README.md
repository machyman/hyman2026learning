# Teaching a course with *Learning with AI*

*For instructors who use the book as the text for a course. Exercise sets cover all nineteen
chapters and Appendix A.*

The book was written as a guide for three readers: students, instructors, and the people who run departments
and universities. It was not written as a textbook, and it has no problem sets. This folder supplies what a
course needs: a choice of course formats, two sample schedules, and exercises for each chapter.

## The course the book fits best: how to teach a course with AI

The best audience for the book is students who will soon teach: graduate students, teaching assistants,
future faculty, and advanced undergraduates who plan to teach. A course on how to teach with AI is the one
course that needs all three parts of the book. A future instructor has to see the course three ways:

- **as a student**, who learns with AI and can let it do the thinking (Part I);
- **as an instructor**, who designs assignments and assessments that still show learning (Part II);
- **as the department sees it**, where policy, standards, and law set limits on any one course (Part III).

The course runs in that order. Students first use AI as learners, so the rules they later write are for tools
they have used. Then each student redesigns a real course, one they may teach or have helped teach. Finally
they test the redesign against their own university's policy. The term ends with each student presenting a
course redesign that has been checked from all three sides.

## Which courses fit

| Course | Audience | Chapters | Length | Fit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **How to teach a course with AI** | Graduate students, teaching assistants, future faculty, advanced undergraduates who plan to teach | All three parts | 14 weeks | **Strongest.** The only course that uses every part of the book, and the one its three viewpoints were written for. |
| **Graduate seminar: learning and research with AI** | Graduate students in any STEM field | Part I, Section 7.7, Appendix A | 8 to 10 weeks, 1 credit | **Strong.** Part I and the research appendix are written for this reader. |
| **First-year seminar: learning with AI** | Entering undergraduates | Part I | 6 to 8 weeks | **Strong**, using the simpler exercises in each set. |

| **Full-semester undergraduate textbook** | Students who do not plan to teach | All | 14 weeks | **Weak.** Too little of the book speaks to this reader for a whole semester. |

has one step of a term project: a redesign of a real course, built up week by week. Every exercise set ends
with a short check, done without AI a week later, because the book's main claim is that scores with AI on do
not show learning.

## The exercises

[How the exercises are built](exercise-design.md) sets out the ten tests every exercise meets and the shape of
each chapter's set. In brief, each set has:

- a map from exercises to the chapter's key points;
- a label at the start of every exercise giving its assignment category (Section 3.4);
- short questions answered from memory, without AI;
- exercises that apply the chapter to the student's own courses;
- exercises that use AI;
- starred exercises for students who want more;
- a check one week later, without AI;
- a weak and a strong response to the main exercise, with notes on what separates them.

**Parts II and III are read from the student's side.** Their exercises do not ask students to act as
instructors or administrators. They ask students to look at how teaching, assessment, and policy shape their
own learning, and to judge what the book proposes from where they sit. In the course on how to teach with AI,
this is deliberate. The term project supplies the instructor's side, built on the
[`instructors/`](../instructors/) and [`institutions/`](../institutions/) toolkits, and the exercises keep
each future instructor answering for the student who will take the course.

## Notes for instructors

- Pick exercises; do not assign every one. A typical week uses the memory questions, one application
  exercise, and one AI exercise.
- Grade the reasoning, not the answer. Each exercise says what the grade rests on.
- The check a week later is the only part that measures learning. Keep it short, keep it without AI, and do
  not skip it.
- If you run a course with these exercises, the author would like to hear what worked and what did not. See
  [FEEDBACK.md](../FEEDBACK.md).
