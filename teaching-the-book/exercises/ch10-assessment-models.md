# Exercises for Chapter 10: Assessment Models

*Part II, the instructor guide, read from the student's side of the desk. Section numbers refer to v6.1.1 of the
book. See [how the exercises are built](../exercise-design.md).*

*Each exercise starts with its assignment category from Section 3.4: NAI (No AI), AIT (AI as Tutor), AIC (AI as Collaborator), or AIS (AI as Object of Study). If your instructor sets a different category, follow theirs.*

## Key points

| | Key point | Section | Exercises |
|---|---|---|---|
| K1 | Automation bias: people working with an aid attend only to what it reports, and miss what it doesn't. People who had seen the aid fail checked its later advice more carefully. | Chapter 10 opening | A1, D1 |
| K2 | The assessment models divide lines of a grade you already have. One grade can then blend what you can do alone with what you can do with AI, and graduate programs and employers will ask about the first. | 10.1 | B1 |
| K3 | Oral-Defense Model: you may use AI more when you can defend the work yourself. Its five segments are summary, core question, audit-and-repair, transfer, and process defense. It grades ownership, not polish, and can use formats beyond speech. | 10.2, 10.2.1, 10.2.3 | A2, B2, C1 |
| K4 | Evidence-of-Thinking Model: in large classes, many small records and checks replace one big defense. A random audit asks ordinary questions and is part of the design, not an accusation. | 10.3, 10.3.5 | A3, C1 |
| K5 | The seeded-error check measures verification: errors of three kinds are planted, you are told how many, and you must explain each one, not just flag it. | 10.3.7 | C2 |
| K6 | Spot checks can be random, stratified, or triggered by a threshold. Each trades fairness against efficiency, and thresholds can be gamed. Reviewing a classmate's work tests understanding in a way your own work cannot. | 10.4, 10.4.2 | D2 |
| K7 | One assisted task, then a later transfer task: same structure, new surface. What remains when the tool is taken away is the measure of what you learned. | 10.5 | A4, E2 |
| K8 | AI may assist an instructor's judgment but must not replace it. Good feedback answers where you are going, how you are going, and where to next. When AI helped grade your work, you are owed three things. | 10.7, 10.7.3 | A4, B3 |

## A. From memory (about 10 minutes)

Answer without notes. Then check each answer in the section the key-points table gives for it, and mark what you missed.

**A1.** *NAI.* In the automation-bias study at the start of the chapter, what did people with the automated aid
miss? What two arrangements made people check more?

**A2.** *NAI.* Name the five segments of an oral defense, in order, and what each one tests.

**A3.** *NAI.* List the ordinary questions a random oral audit asks (Section 10.3.5).

**A4.** *NAI.* What makes a good transfer task, and what goes wrong if it changes too little or too much? When AI
has helped grade your work, what three things are you owed?

## B. In your own courses

**B1. What your grade will say.** *NAI. About 15 minutes.* Write out the grade formula of one course. Mark each line
as independent work, AI-assisted work, or both. If a graduate program or an employer asked what you can do on your
own, which lines would answer? What would the final grade hide?
*Submit:* the marked formula and three sentences.
*Graded on:* whether each mark matches the syllabus.

**B2. How you show what you know.** *NAI. About 10 minutes.* Section 10.2.3 says a defense grades ownership, not
polish, and that "oral" need not mean speaking only. In a few sentences, say how you show understanding best: at a
board, in writing, with a diagram, in steps. If a live format would not show your understanding well, draft a
two-sentence request for another format. You never need to give a reason you would rather keep private.
*Submit:* your sentences, and the request if you wrote one.
*Graded on:* whether you name a concrete format.

**B3. Sort your feedback.** *NAI. About 15 minutes.* Take one piece of written feedback you received this term. Sort
each comment by the three questions in Section 10.7: where am I going, how am I going, where to next. Does it give
hints or rewrite your work for you? If you know AI helped write it, were you told?
*Submit:* the sorted comments, paraphrased, and two sentences on what the feedback asked you to do next.
*Graded on:* the sorting.

## C. With AI

**C1. Rehearse your own defense.** *AIT. About 45 minutes, plus 15 minutes a week later.*

1. Choose a recent assignment of your own, one the course allowed AI on. Do not upload it. You will describe it.
2. Open an AI session with this prompt: "You are examining me on my own work, one question at a time, in five
   parts: I summarize; you ask one core question; you give me a short passage about my topic with one flaw for me
   to find and fix; you ask one transfer question about a nearby case; you ask how I used AI and how I checked it.
   Do not answer for me or hint unless I ask."
3. Answer each part from memory, aloud or typed, without looking at your work.
4. Grade each answer yourself: *owned*, *partly owned*, or *not owned*. Say why in one line each.
5. A week later, without AI, answer again the question you graded lowest.

*Submit:* a shared link to the session, your five grades with reasons, and your second answer. Add a one-line
disclosure.
*Graded on:* how honestly you graded yourself, and what changed between your two answers.

**C2. Seeded errors, for each other.** *AIS. Pairs. About 45 minutes.*

1. Each partner asks an AI for a full solution to a different problem from your course, one AI is allowed on for
   practice.
2. Check the solution. Then plant exactly three errors of the three kinds in Section 10.3.7: one a careful reader
   would catch, one that takes knowing the material, and one that takes checking a source, a table, or a value.
   Keep your own list.
3. Swap. Tell your partner there are three. Each partner finds and explains every error they can, without AI.
4. Compare with the lists. Score your detection rate.

*Submit:* your seeded solution, your list, your findings on your partner's, and three sentences on which kind of
error you missed and why. Add a one-line disclosure.
*Graded on:* the explanations, not the count.

## D. Going further ★

**D1. Your own automation bias.** *NAI. One week.* Each time you accept an answer from an AI tool, note what you
checked yourself and what you took on trust. At the end of the week, write one page comparing your notes with the
study at the start of the chapter. When did seeing the tool fail change how you checked it?

**D2. Which audit would you choose?** *NAI. One page.* Read the three sampling strategies in Section 10.4.2 as the
student being sampled. Which is fairest to you? Which could a student game, and what would gaming cost that
student in learning? Then say whether you would rather review a classmate's work or be audited yourself, and which
would teach you more.

## E. One week later (about 10 minutes)

**E1.** *NAI.* Without notes, list the five segments of an oral defense and the questions of a random audit.

**E2.** *NAI.* Think of something you did with AI help this week. Write a transfer task for it: same structure, new
surface (Section 10.5). Then do it, without AI.

## What a strong answer looks like: C1

Two condensed responses to C1 from a biology lab course. Each student rehearsed a defense of a report explaining
why an enzyme's reaction rate rose from 20°C to 40°C and fell sharply by 60°C.

**A weak response**

> *Summary:* The rate went up and then down because of temperature.
>
> *Core question (why does the rate rise at first?):* Because enzymes work better when warm.
>
> *Audit-and-repair (passage: "At 60°C the enzyme's activation energy increases, so the rate drops"):* This looks
> right.
>
> *Transfer (an enzyme from a hot-spring bacterium):* It would work the same.
>
> *Process:* I used AI to help write the discussion.
>
> *Self-grades:* owned, owned, owned, owned, owned.
>
> *A week later:* (not done)

**A strong response**

> *Summary:* The rate rose with temperature, peaked near 40°C, and fell by 60°C. The fall is denaturation.
>
> *Core question:* Below the peak, molecules collide more often and with more energy, so more reactions happen per
> second. I said this slowly; I had to rebuild it.
>
> *Audit-and-repair:* I accepted the passage on first read. On the second I caught it: the enzyme does not raise the
> activation energy. Heat changes the enzyme's shape, the active site no longer fits the substrate, and the
> catalysis is lost. That loss is denaturation, and it is why the drop is sharp.
>
> *Transfer:* Its optimum should sit higher, because its proteins evolved to hold their shape in hot water. Taq
> polymerase, from a hot-spring bacterium, is used in PCR near 72°C.
>
> *Process:* AI explained collision theory before I wrote the discussion; the wording and the data analysis are
> mine. I checked its explanation against the lab manual.
>
> *Self-grades:* summary owned; core question partly owned (I knew the claim, not the reason); audit-and-repair
> partly owned (I missed it once); transfer owned; process owned.
>
> *A week later, core question:* Raising the temperature raises the share of collisions with enough energy to
> react, so the rate rises, until the enzyme loses its shape. I could say it without stopping.
>
> *Disclosure:* An AI examined me in this rehearsal (link above); it gave no answers.

**What separates them**

- The weak answers name the topic. The strong answers give the reason, and say where the student had to stop and
  rebuild it.
- The weak student accepts the flawed passage. The strong student admits missing it once, then names the real
  mechanism. That admission is the audit doing its job.
- The weak self-grades are all "owned", and nothing is repeated. The strong ones find two weak spots and repair the
  worst a week later.

## For instructors

- **Minimum week:** A1 to A4, B1, and C1, with its follow-up and E1 a week later. About two hours of student time.
- **C1 is practice for the real thing.** It never replaces an oral defense or audit. Students describe their work
  rather than upload it (exercise-design.md, Disclosure and privacy). Share your own example audit questions, as
  Section 10.2.3 advises, and students can rehearse with them.
- **C2** uses AI output the students check first, so the seeded errors are deliberate. Section 9.3.5 notes that an
  AI asked to plant errors in its own work may pick easy ones, which is why students plant them here.
- **B2 must never require a student to disclose an accommodation.** It asks only how a student shows understanding.
- **B3** may surface feedback written with AI that students were not told about. Section 10.7.1 asks instructors to
  say so; treat a student's question about it as fair.
- **Looking ahead:** Chapter 11 sets out what happens when work exceeds its label, and the fair response sequence
  that follows. D2 pairs with D2 in the [Chapter 8 set](ch08-the-instructors-trilemma.md).
