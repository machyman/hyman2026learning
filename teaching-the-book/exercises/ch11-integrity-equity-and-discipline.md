# Exercises for Chapter 11: Integrity, Equity, and Discipline

*Part II, the instructor guide, read from the student's side of the desk. Section numbers refer to v6.1.1 of the
book. See [how the exercises are built](../exercise-design.md).*

*Each exercise starts with its assignment category from Section 3.4: NAI (No AI), AIT (AI as Tutor), AIC (AI as Collaborator), or AIS (AI as Object of Study). If your instructor sets a different category, follow theirs.*

## Key points

| | Key point | Section | Exercises |
|---|---|---|---|
| K1 | Weak AI use asks the tool to solve rather than to hint, then submits without checking. An audit a week later exposes it: the student cannot explain the setup or adapt it. A proof can be correct without being yours. | 11.1 | A1, C1 |
| K2 | A detector score cannot carry an integrity decision. Its false positives fall on non-native writers, and possibly on others whose writing departs from the norm; its false negatives go to anyone who edits the output once. Instructors should use several sources of evidence and a fair conversation instead. | 11.2, 11.3.4 | A2, D1 |
| K3 | The integrity rule binds both sides of the desk. The translation test is the positive standard for students and instructors alike. | 11.2 | D2 |
| K4 | The AIT line is crossed gradually, inside one conversation. The check you can run yourself: close the tool and rebuild the key step from a blank page. If you cannot, the help became production. | 11.2.1 | B1, C1 |
| K5 | When work exceeds its label, two questions are kept apart: what you can do, settled by measuring again, and whether you misrepresented your use, settled by a fair response sequence. A first conversation is usually meant to return you to the work. | 11.2.1, 11.2.2 | A3, B2 |
| K6 | Privacy, access, and equity: upload nothing that identifies other people, design for the free baseline, and spread assessment across formats. AI help with phrasing that you then write in your own words is AIT; once AI wording enters the work, it is AIC and must be disclosed. | 11.3 | A4, C2 |
| K7 | Each discipline has its own core competence, its own checks, and its own typical failure, such as code you cannot explain or a test whose assumptions do not hold. | 11.4 | B3 |
| K8 | Five rubric anchors: correctness, reasoning visibility, verification discipline, communication, and integrity. In an AIC rubric, verification, ownership, and disclosure are graded separately. | 11.5 | E2 |

## A. From memory (about 10 minutes)

Answer without notes. Then check each answer in the section the key-points table gives for it, and mark what you missed.

**A1.** *NAI.* In Dialogue 7, what two mistakes did the student make? What two questions at the audit exposed
them, and what course rule did the submission break?

**A2.** *NAI.* Why can't a detector score carry an integrity decision on its own? Name the two directions of error
and who bears each. Then list four of the eight sources of evidence Section 11.2 names instead.

**A3.** *NAI.* List the six steps of the fair response sequence. What is a first conversation about a concern
usually for?

**A4.** *NAI.* When is AI help with English phrasing AIT, and when does it become AIC? What do you owe in the second
case?

## B. In your own courses

**B1. The blank-page check.** *NAI. About 20 minutes.* Pick an assignment this term where you used AI as a tutor.
Close every tool and every note. Rebuild its key step from a blank page: the setup, the central argument, or the
core paragraph (Section 11.2.1). Then compare it with what you submitted.
*Submit:* your rebuild, and two sentences: could you do it, and if not, where did the help cross into production?
If your disclosure now looks incomplete, say what you would add.
*Graded on:* whether the rebuild is honest about what you could and could not do. A failed rebuild costs nothing.

**B2. What happens if a question is raised?** *NAI. About 15 minutes.* Read your course's or your university's
academic integrity policy. Does it describe steps like the fair response sequence in Section 11.2.2? Who would
contact you, and what could you bring? For your most recent submission, list which of the evidence sources in
Section 11.2 you could produce today.
*Submit:* half a page.
*Graded on:* whether each point cites the policy or names a real piece of evidence.

**B3. Your field's failure.** *NAI. About 15 minutes.* Find the overlay in Section 11.4 nearest your field. State
its typical failure in your own words. Describe a time you came close to it in your own work, and the check that
caught it, or would have.
*Submit:* one paragraph.
*Graded on:* whether the example is specific and the check would actually catch the failure.

## C. With AI

**C1. Run Dialogue 7 on yourself.** *AIS, then AIT. About 60 minutes.*

1. Choose a problem from your course you have not solved yet, one the course allows AI on for practice.
2. *AIS.* Ask an AI to solve it, as the student in Dialogue 7 did. Save the solution. Read it once.
3. Close it. Without AI or notes, answer the two audit questions as they apply to your problem: why the setup is
   written the way it is, and what would change if one given were different.
4. *AIT.* Start a new session. Ask for hints only, never the solution, and work the problem yourself.
5. Answer the two audit questions again, without AI or notes.

*Submit:* the AI's solution, your answers from step 3, a link to the session in step 4, your answers from step 5,
and half a page on what changed. Add a one-line disclosure.
*Graded on:* the difference between your two sets of answers, and how honestly you report the first.

**C2. A self-check, not a verdict.** *AIT. About 30 minutes.* Write a fresh paragraph for practice, not graded work:
for example, explain a concept from your course to a first-year student. Ask an AI to flag clichéd phrases,
repetition, and unclear sentences, and not to rewrite anything (the self-check use in Section 11.2.3). Revise the
paragraph yourself. If you write in English as an additional language, ask also about phrasing, then write the
changes in your own words (Section 11.3.4).
*Submit:* both versions, the AI's flags, and a one-line disclosure saying which rule applied: tutoring only, or AI
wording in the final version.
*Graded on:* whether the revisions are yours, and whether the disclosure matches what happened.

## D. Going further ★

**D1. Detectors from where you sit.** *NAI. One page.* Find what your university says about AI detectors. Read it
against Sections 11.2 and 11.3.4. Would a student whose writing departs from the norm, a multilingual writer or a
neurodivergent writer, be protected from a decision based on a score? If the policy says nothing, say what you
would want it to say, and why.

**D2. Both sides of the desk.** *NAI. One page.* Section 11.2 describes a student complaint about an instructor who
used AI for lecture materials under a syllabus that banned AI for students. What would you want to know about AI
use in the materials and feedback you receive, and why? Use the translation test as the standard for both sides.
Write about courses in general, not about any instructor you know.

## E. One week later (about 10 minutes)

**E1.** *NAI.* Without notes, list the six steps of the fair response sequence, and the blank-page check from
Section 11.2.1.

**E2.** *NAI.* Without notes, name the five rubric anchors. Score your last submission on reasoning visibility and
verification discipline, using the four levels in Section 11.5.1.

## What a strong answer looks like: C1

Two condensed responses to C1 from a calculus course. The problem: an open-top box with a square base must hold
32,000 cm³; find the dimensions that use the least material.

**A weak response**

> *AI solution:* Base x, height h. x²h = 32,000, so h = 32,000/x². Material S = x² + 4xh = x² + 128,000/x.
> S′ = 2x − 128,000/x² = 0 gives x = 40 cm, h = 20 cm.
>
> *Audit before (why this setup? what if the box had a lid?):* The AI substituted for h. With a lid it would be
> about the same.
>
> *Hint-only session:* (skipped; I already had the answer)
>
> *Audit after:* Same as before.
>
> *Half page:* The AI's solution was correct, so the exercise showed I can use AI well.
>
> *Disclosure:* AI used.

**A strong response**

> *AI solution:* as above, x = 40 cm and h = 20 cm.
>
> *Audit before:* Why x² + 4xh becomes x² + 128,000/x: I didn't know why that step was allowed. With a lid: "the
> same", which was a guess.
>
> *Hint-only session:* I asked what to do with two unknowns. The hint: "Which fact relates x and h?" I used the
> volume to write h in terms of x, so S became a function of x alone, and found x = 40 myself. I checked that S″ is
> positive there.
>
> *Audit after:* The volume constraint turns a two-variable problem into a one-variable problem, which is why the
> substitution comes first. With a lid, S = 2x² + 4xh = 2x² + 128,000/x. Then S′ = 4x − 128,000/x² = 0 gives
> x³ = 32,000, so x ≈ 31.7 cm, and h = 32,000/x² ≈ 31.7 cm: a cube. That makes sense, because with a lid the top
> and bottom are the same and nothing favors one direction.
>
> *Half page:* After reading the AI's solution I could have submitted it and failed both audit questions. The
> hint-only session took longer, and it is the reason I can now answer the transfer question. This is the
> blank-page check of Section 11.2.1 run as an experiment.
>
> *Disclosure:* An AI wrote the step 2 solution, which I studied but did not submit. In step 4 it gave one hint
> (link above). The answers are mine.

**What separates them**

- The weak response skips the hint-only session, which is the only part that builds anything.
- The weak audit answers name what the AI did. The strong ones say why: the constraint removes a variable.
- The strong transfer answer is worked out and checked against a reason (symmetry). "About the same" is the
  failure in Dialogue 7.
- The weak half page calls a correct AI answer a success for the student. The strong one separates the two.

## For instructors

- **Minimum week:** A1 to A4, B1, and C1, then E1 a week later. About two hours of student time.
- **C1 is the anchor.** It follows Dialogue 7 on the student's own problem, then repairs it under AIT. Grade the
  change in the audit answers, not the final answer.
- **B1 may lead a student to correct a disclosure.** Treat that as Section 11.2.1 does: a question of measurement,
  not penalty. A course that punishes the student who comes forward teaches the opposite lesson.
- **C2 uses a fresh practice paragraph**, never graded work under review, so nothing private is uploaded. It makes
  the AIT and AIC line for language help concrete (Section 11.3.4).
- **D1 and D2 ask for a student's view of policy**, not a verdict on any person. Keep the discussion general.
- **Looking ahead:** Chapter 12 covers how a teaching team agrees on what a strong answer looks like. The rubric
  anchors in E2 return in the [Chapter 13 set](ch13-the-instructors-toolkit.md).
