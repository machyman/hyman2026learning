# Exercises for Chapter 9: The Framework and Assignment Design

*Part II, the instructor guide, read from the student's side of the desk. Section numbers refer to v6.1.1 of the
book. See [how the exercises are built](../exercise-design.md).*

*Each exercise starts with its assignment category from Section 3.4: NAI (No AI), AIT (AI as Tutor), AIC (AI as Collaborator), or AIS (AI as Object of Study). If your instructor sets a different category, follow theirs.*

## Key points

| | Key point | Section | Exercises |
|---|---|---|---|
| K1 | Design can protect your will to learn but cannot create it. You attempt the struggle only where a wrong first try feels safe. Writing about why a topic matters to you has helped students who did not expect to do well; an AI-written version keeps the form and drops the substance. | Chapter 9 opening, 9.3 | B1 |
| K2 | The CAT framework assesses three things: Core Competence, AI-assisted Practice, and Trustworthiness. AI skill never makes up for missing the core goals of a course. | 9.1 | A1, B2 |
| K3 | Trustworthiness shows in five answers: what AI contributed; what you accepted, rejected, or revised; how you checked; which sources you verified yourself; and what you can explain without AI. | 9.1.3 | A2, C1 |
| K4 | Each category follows from what the assignment is for. The line between AIT and AIC is one you can check: AI wording, code, or worked solutions either enter what you hand in or they do not. Introductory courses lean toward NAI and AIT because students cannot yet verify AI output. | 9.2, 9.2.7 | A3, B2 |
| K5 | A category label tells you what is allowed but does not change the task. Ask what happens when a student ignores it: the answer comes from the assignment, not the syllabus. | 9.2.5 | D2 |
| K6 | Plan-Use-Verify is one pass of the Learning Spiral collected as evidence: a plan before AI, a record of the use, and a check after. It works when the records are short and the plan comes first. | 9.3, 9.3.1 | C1, E1 |
| K7 | A problem written in spiral form gives you somewhere to go when stuck: estimate, compute, ask AI for the flaw rather than the answer, check a second way, and predict what changes. A scope line keeps the tutor inside your course. | 9.3.2, 9.3.5 | C2 |
| K8 | In the high-school mathematics trial, an AI that simply answered raised practice scores and lowered exam scores. A tutor that gave hints, with the worked solutions in its prompt, raised practice scores with no exam loss. | 9.3 | A4 |
| K9 | Polish and learning are independent. Conditions that improve performance now can fail to build learning that lasts. Students who route questions to AI instead of each other lose the peer help much learning has depended on. | 9.3, 9.3.7 | D1, E2 |

## A. From memory (about 10 minutes)

Answer without notes. Then check each answer in the section the key-points table gives for it, and mark what you missed.

**A1.** *NAI.* Name the three dimensions of the CAT framework and say what each asks of you. What is the
framework's one non-negotiable feature?

**A2.** *NAI.* List the five questions from Section 9.1.3 that show a student's responsible judgment.

**A3.** *NAI.* For each of the four categories, give the AI's role. What line separates AIT from AIC, and why can a
student check it?

**A4.** *NAI.* In the trial by Bastani and colleagues, what did an unrestricted AI do to practice and exam
performance? What did the guardrailed tutor do? What did the tutor have in its prompt, and why did that matter?

## B. In your own courses

**B1. Why this matters to me.** *NAI. About 15 minutes.* Pick a topic you are studying this week. In one paragraph,
write how it connects to your own life, your plans, or a problem you care about. Be specific: name the place, the
job, or the question. Section 9.3 explains why this has to be yours: a chatbot will write a good-sounding
paragraph in seconds, and the paragraph does nothing for you.
*Submit:* the paragraph.
*Graded on:* whether it names a specific connection. Feelings about the topic are welcome; none is required.

**B2. Read your course's categories.** *NAI. About 20 minutes.* For one course, list its graded assignments. Give
each the category its rules imply, and say what each is for: Core Competence, AI-assisted Practice, or
Trustworthiness. Does the mix fit the course's level, as Section 9.2.7 describes? Mark any assignment whose AI rule
you could not state with confidence. For one of those, write the question you would ask, naming the role you want
AI to play (as in the [Chapter 6 set](ch06-judgment-and-limits.md), B3).
*Submit:* the list and the question.
*Graded on:* whether each category is backed by the assignment's own wording.

## C. With AI

**C1. Three short records.** *AIT, or AIC if your course sets it. About 60 minutes.*

Use a real assignment from this term that allows AI, or a past one your instructor approves.

1. *Plan,* before any AI use. Write a short plan: your setup, the method you intend, and one prediction about the
   result. Note the time.
2. *Use.* Use AI within the assignment's rules. Keep a short log: what you asked, and what came back, in summary.
   Do not paste the whole transcript; save a shared link instead.
3. *Verify.* Check the result with a method that does not depend on the AI: a test, a second method, a source, a
   limiting case. Record what you accepted, changed, or rejected.
4. Answer the five questions from Section 9.1.3, one line each.

*Submit:* the three records, one page at most, the five answers, and a one-line disclosure.
*Graded on:* whether the plan came before the AI, and whether the check stands on its own. Length earns nothing
(Section 9.3.1).

**C2. Recast a problem.** *AIT. About 40 minutes.* Take a single-answer problem from your course, one you have not
solved yet. Rewrite it for yourself in the spiral form of Section 9.3.2: estimate first and say what the estimate
assumes; compute; if your estimate is far off, ask an AI tutor for the flaw in your reasoning, not the answer;
check a second way; then predict what changes when one input changes. Open the AI session with a one-line scope
note naming what your course has covered (Section 9.3.5). Then work it.
*Submit:* the original problem, your recast, your work, the scope line, a link to the AI exchange if you used one,
and a one-line disclosure.
*Graded on:* the estimate and the prediction. Was each made before the computation, and what did you learn when it
was wrong?

## D. Going further ★

**D1. A classmate first.** *NAI. Two weeks.* Once a week, take a question to a classmate or a study group before
you take it to AI. Note what each source gave you, and what you gave back. In one page, set what you found beside
the finding in Section 9.3 that students now route questions to AI instead of to each other. What did the person
give you that the tool did not?

**D2. When the label is ignored.** *NAI. One page.* For two assignments in your courses, answer the question from
Section 9.2.5: what happens when a student ignores the AI rule? Does the assignment itself still show what the
student can do, or does only the label stand in the way? Which of the two would you learn more from, and why? Use
the polish and learning grid from Section 9.3.7.

## E. One week later (about 10 minutes)

**E1.** *NAI.* Without notes, name the three records of Plan-Use-Verify, and one way each can break.

**E2.** *NAI.* Without notes, sketch the grid of polished output against demonstrated learning. Place your last
submitted assignment on it, and say what evidence put it there.

## What a strong answer looks like: C1

Two condensed responses to C1 from an introductory programming course. The assignment, under AIT, asked for a
Python function that returns the median of a list of numbers. AI could explain and critique but could not write
code.

**A weak response**

> *Plan:* Sort the list with `nums.sort()`. If the length is even, average the two middle values; if odd, take the
> middle one. If the list is empty, raise `ValueError`.
>
> *Use:* (three pages of pasted conversation)
>
> *Verify:* I ran it on a few lists and it worked.
>
> *Five questions:* AI helped me understand medians. I accepted its advice. I checked by running the code. No
> sources. I can explain all of it.
>
> *Disclosure:* AI used as a tutor.

**A strong response**

> *Plan, 7:40 pm:* Sort, then take the middle. Even length: average of the two middle values. Prediction:
> `[4, 1, 3, 2]` gives 2.5.
>
> *Use, 7:55 pm:* Asked: "What edge cases does this plan miss? Don't write code." It named the empty list, and
> warned that sorting in place changes the caller's list. Link above.
>
> *Verify:* Tests: `[3, 1, 2]` gives 2; `[4, 1, 3, 2]` gives 2.5, as predicted. For the empty list I raise
> `ValueError`, because the assignment says invalid input raises an error; the AI had suggested returning `None`. I
> then printed the input list after the call. With `nums.sort()` it came back sorted, so I switched to
> `sorted(nums)`, which returns a new list, and the input came back unchanged.
>
> *Five questions:* AI contributed two edge cases. I accepted the mutation warning after my own test showed it, and
> rejected returning `None`. I checked with four test cases and a before-and-after print of the input. No outside
> sources. I can explain every line, including why `sorted` and not `sort`.
>
> *Disclosure:* I used an AI tutor once, for the edge-case critique above; the plan, code, and tests are mine.

**What separates them**

- The weak plan already contains the empty-list rule, which reads like an answer learned later. The strong plan
  is timed before the AI and makes a prediction the tests then confirm.
- The weak response pastes a transcript; the strong one logs the one question that mattered (Section 9.3.1).
- The weak check is "it worked". The strong check tests the input as well as the output, and that test, not the
  AI, settles the mutation point.
- The weak answers to the five questions could describe any assignment. The strong ones name what was accepted
  and what was rejected, and why.

## For instructors

- **Minimum week:** A1 to A4, B2, and C1, then E1 a week later. About two hours of student time.
- **C1 is the anchor.** Grade it at a glance, as Section 9.3.1 advises. Look for a plan written before the AI use
  and a check that stands on its own. Do not reward length or transcripts.
- **B1 is the relevance-writing design** of Section 9.3. The book calls it promising, not settled. Grade it only
  on specificity, and never on what a student chooses to share.
- **C2 is study design, not course design.** Students recast a problem for their own practice. Nothing here asks
  them to set a course's rules.
- **Privacy:** C1 and C2 use the student's own work on assignments that allow AI. Shared links replace pasted
  transcripts. Nothing private is uploaded.
- **Looking ahead:** Chapter 10's assessment models are where the Trustworthiness questions are asked in person.
  D2 pairs with D2 in the [Chapter 8 set](ch08-the-instructors-trilemma.md).
