# Exercises for Chapter 15: The Framework and Vocabulary

*Part III, the departmental and institutional guide, read from the student's side of the desk. Section numbers
refer to v6.1.1 of the book. See [how the exercises are built](../exercise-design.md). Chapter 15 restates
vocabulary you met in Parts I and II, so this set is short. It asks one new question: how would your program show
that you learned what it says you learned?*

*Each exercise starts with its assignment category from Section 3.4: NAI (No AI), AIT (AI as Tutor), AIC (AI as Collaborator), or AIS (AI as Object of Study). If your instructor sets a different category, follow theirs.*

## Key points

| | Key point | Section | Exercises |
|---|---|---|---|
| K1 | The CAT framework names three kinds of learning evidence: Core Competence, AI-assisted Practice, and Trustworthiness. They can diverge, so good policy does not collapse them into one score. One assessment can serve more than one. | 15.1 | A1, B1, D1 |
| K2 | Accreditors ask whether an institution can show that students achieve the outcomes it claims. Where an outcome requires independent competence, the institution must show it was assessed independently of AI. Assessment design and accreditation reporting are one task. | 15.1.1 | C1 |
| K3 | A category names the most permissive role AI plays in the submitted work. Once AI wording, code, or worked solutions enter it, the work is AIC. Where a task mixes roles, the instructor should say so. | 15.2 | A2 |
| K4 | The deciding question for the assessment model is whether the teaching team can talk with each student about the work. The table in Section 15.3 gives each course context a center of gravity, not an exclusive choice. | 15.3 | A3, B2 |

## A. From memory (about 10 minutes)

Answer without notes. Then check each answer in the section the key-points table gives for it, and mark what you missed.

**A1.** *NAI.* Name the three CAT dimensions. Give an example of a student strong on one and weak on another.

**A2.** *NAI.* A session starts with an AI explaining an approach and ends with it helping draft the answer. What
category is the submitted work, and why?

**A3.** *NAI.* What question decides the assessment model? Which model does the table in Section 15.3 recommend for
a large lecture, a capstone, and an online course?

## B. In your own courses

**B1. Map your major.** *NAI. About 40 minutes.* Take four required courses in your major. For each, name one
assessment that measures Core Competence, one that develops AI-assisted Practice, and one that evaluates
Trustworthiness. Write "none found" where you find none.
*Submit:* a four-row table and one sentence on where your program checks what you can do on your own.
*Graded on:* whether each entry points to a syllabus or an assignment.

**B2. Your courses in the table.** *NAI. About 15 minutes.* Place three of your courses in the table in Section
15.3. Does each run the recommended model? If not, what does it do instead, and does that still let the teaching
team see your thinking?
*Submit:* three short rows.
*Graded on:* whether each row names the evidence the course actually collects.

## C. With AI

**C1. From outcomes to evidence.** *NAI, then AIT for the critique. About 60 minutes.*

1. Find the published learning outcomes for your program, in the catalog or on the department's site.
2. Without AI, sort each outcome into Core Competence, AI-assisted Practice, or Trustworthiness. Split an outcome
   where it mixes them. For each part, name an assessment in your program that shows it.
3. Ask an AI to critique your sort: which outcomes would it place differently, and why? Ask it not to rewrite
   your sort.
4. Decide for yourself which suggestions to take. Then name the one outcome whose independent evidence is
   weakest.

*Submit:* your first sort, the AI's critique (or a shared link), your revised sort, a paragraph on the weakest
outcome, and a one-line disclosure.
*Graded on:* whether every outcome is tied to a real assessment, and whether the weakest one is named with a
reason.

## D. Going further ★

**D1. One score or three?** *NAI. One page.* Section 15.1 says good policy does not collapse the CAT dimensions into
one score. Your transcript reports one grade per course. Sketch a course grade or a transcript entry that kept them
apart. Who would read it, what would it cost to produce, and what would it tell an employer that a letter grade
does not?

## E. One week later (about 10 minutes)

**E1.** *NAI.* Without notes, name the CAT dimensions and the rule that makes work AIC. Then name the assessment in
your major that best shows what you can do on your own.

## What a strong answer looks like: C1

Two condensed entries for one outcome from a chemistry program: "Students will analyze chemical data using
appropriate statistical and computational tools."

**A weak response**

> *Sort:* Core Competence. Shown in the lab reports.
>
> *Weakest outcome:* None. All outcomes are covered by the courses.

**A strong response**

> *Sort, split:* Building a calibration curve and stating its uncertainty is Core Competence, shown in the lab
> practical, which is NAI. Running the regression in software is AI-assisted Practice, shown in the AIC lab
> reports. Checking the software's slope against a hand estimate, and saying so, is Trustworthiness, shown in the
> reports' verification section.
>
> *AI critique:* it suggested filing the whole outcome under AI-assisted Practice. I kept the split. The outcome
> says "analyze," and an accreditor would ask whether students can analyze without the tool (Section 15.1.1).
>
> *Weakest outcome:* this one. The lab reports are take-home, and nothing in the program checks that a student can
> read a residual plot unaided. The practical tests the calculation, not the interpretation.

**What separates them**

- The weak sort names one dimension for an outcome that mixes three, and the evidence it names is take-home work
  AI could produce.
- The strong sort splits the outcome and ties each part to a real assessment.
- The strong response took the AI's critique seriously and declined it, with a reason grounded in the chapter.
- "None" is rarely true. The strong response finds a real gap: interpretation is never checked unaided.

## For instructors

- **Minimum week:** A1 to A3, B1, and C1, then E1 a week later. About two hours of student time.
- **C1 is the anchor.** Students need published outcomes. If their program publishes none, use the institution's
  general education outcomes, or a related program's, and say so.
- **No accreditation knowledge is assumed.** Section 15.1.1 gives what students need.
- **Privacy:** C1 sends an AI only the student's sort of published outcomes, never graded work.
- **D1 works well as a class discussion** after C1, since the gaps students find are the case for reporting the
  dimensions separately.
- **Looking ahead:** the [Chapter 16 set](ch16-standards-and-requirements.md) reads the standards a student should
  be able to expect: independent competence, disclosure, privacy, equity, and a fair integrity procedure.
