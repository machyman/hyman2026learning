# Exercises for Chapter 14: The Institution's Trilemma

*Part III, the departmental and institutional guide, read from the student's side of the desk. Section numbers
refer to v6.1.1 of the book. See [how the exercises are built](../exercise-design.md). These exercises ask what
your university owes you and what your degree will certify. They never ask you to write policy as if you held
the job.*

*Each exercise starts with its assignment category from Section 3.4: NAI (No AI), AIT (AI as Tutor), AIC (AI as Collaborator), or AIS (AI as Object of Study). If your instructor sets a different category, follow theirs.*

## Key points

| | Key point | Section | Exercises |
|---|---|---|---|
| K1 | A policy based only on prohibition is brittle, and one based only on permission is too vague. Every course still has to answer five questions: what AI use is allowed, what you must do without it, how you disclose, how AI-assisted work is graded, and how suspected misuse is handled fairly. | 14.1 | B1 |
| K2 | The framework names seven outcomes an institution should be able to show. Its test is plain: the policy works only if it improves clarity, learning, fairness, and trust. | 14.2 | B1 |
| K3 | Narrowing. In a controlled experiment, AI story ideas made each story rated better and the whole collection more alike. Students who fear being judged as AI can also write more plainly than they can. Neither effect shows in grades or integrity referrals. | 14.3 | A2, B3, C1 |
| K4 | Two cheap checks make narrowing visible: compare topics against a cohort from before AI, and record your own ideas before consulting any tool. The usual fix is to vary the starting point, not to restrict the tool. | 14.3 | B3, C1 |
| K5 | The Institution's Trilemma: institutional sustainability, what the credential certifies, and the educational mission. Any two can be held at the cost of the third. Unlike a student, the institution cannot resolve it by choosing: it must certify every student under the same degree. | 14.4 | A1, B2, C2 |
| K6 | A policy that only forbids passing off AI work as your own defends honesty but not understanding. You could follow it exactly and still graduate without learning what the degree says you learned. | 14.4.1 | A3, B1 |
| K7 | Which corner pulls hardest depends on the type of institution, and so does the failure mode most likely to appear. | 14.4.2 | B2 |
| K8 | A budget that rewards enrollment can push departments, and instructors, toward the most permissive policy on offer. Only the level that sets the budget can fix it. | 14.4.3 | D1 |
| K9 | A competence costume is AI-polished material that signals more engagement than the work behind it supports. The translation test applies to instructors and students alike: restate the material in your own words and with your own examples. | 14.5 | A4, D2 |
| K10 | Eight guiding principles, from "learning remains the center" to "faculty judgment remains human." | 14.6 | B1 |

## A. From memory (about 10 minutes)

Answer without notes. Then check each answer in the section the key-points table gives for it, and mark what you missed.

**A1.** *NAI.* Name the three corners of the Institution's Trilemma. For each edge, name the failure mode and the
corner it gives up.

**A2.** *NAI.* Describe the two paths to narrowing in Section 14.3. Why does neither show up where a department
usually looks?

**A3.** *NAI.* What does a policy that only forbids passing off AI work as your own leave undefended, and why does
that matter to you?

**A4.** *NAI.* What is a competence costume? What standard does Section 14.5 propose for instructors and students
alike?

## B. In your own courses

**B1. Read your university's policy.** *NAI. About 30 minutes.* Find the AI policy your institution or
department publishes. Check it against the five questions in Section 14.1 and the eight principles in Section 14.6.
Which does it answer, and which does it leave to each course? Does it stop at honesty (Section 14.4.1)? If you
cannot find a policy, say where you looked. That is a finding too.
*Submit:* a table with one row per question or principle, each entry quoting or pointing to the policy.
*Graded on:* whether each entry points to the policy text rather than summarizing it from memory.

**B2. Which kind of institution is yours?** *NAI. About 20 minutes.* Using Section 14.4.2, place your institution
among the four types, or name the mix. Which corner pulls hardest? Which failure mode would a student notice first,
and what would it look like from your seat?
*Submit:* one paragraph.
*Graded on:* whether the reasoning rests on features of the institution, not on any person.

**B3. Your ideas first.** *NAI. Five minutes, then one line later.* Before your next project proposal or paper
topic, write three candidate ideas without consulting any tool. Keep the list. After the topic is chosen, note
which idea you took and where it came from.
*Submit:* the list and the note.
*Graded on:* whether the list was written first. The idea you choose need not come from it.

## C. With AI

**C1. The narrowing test.** *NAI, then AIS. About 50 minutes, as a whole class or in groups of eight or more.*

1. **Your own ideas (NAI).** Each student writes three project ideas on a topic the class shares, without any
   tool. Hand them in before step 2.
2. **The AI's ideas (AIS).** Each student gives the same AI the same prompt, written by the class, and records its
   first three ideas. Record the tool and the date.
3. **Count.** Agree on a rule for when two ideas count as the same, and write it down before counting. Count the
   distinct ideas among the students' lists and among the AI's lists.
4. **Interpret.** Compare your result with the finding in Section 14.3. Say what your class sample can and
   cannot show.

*Submit:* your own three ideas, the AI's three, the class tally with its counting rule, and a paragraph of
interpretation with a one-line disclosure.
*Graded on:* whether the counting rule was fixed before counting, and whether the conclusion stays within what
the sample can show.

**C2. The replacement argument.** *AIS. About 30 minutes.* Ask an AI to make the strongest case that your
university could replace most instruction in one of your courses with AI tutors at lower cost. Then identify which
corner of the trilemma its case gives up, using Section 14.4.3. Write your reply in one paragraph of your own: what
would you lose, and what would your degree still certify?
*Submit:* the AI's case (or a shared link), your paragraph, and a one-line disclosure.
*Graded on:* whether your reply names a corner and says what is lost, rather than restating the chapter.

## D. Going further ★

**D1. The budget drift.** *NAI. One page.* Section 14.4.3 describes how an enrollment-based budget can push
departments toward the most permissive AI policy. Explain the mechanism in your own words. How would students
notice it, course by course? What could a student government reasonably ask the institution to do about it? Write
about structures, not about any department or person.

**D2. The translation test, both ways.** *NAI. One page.* Should instructors who use AI for course materials be
held to the same translation test as students? Use the case in Section 14.5 and the eighth principle in Section
14.6. Argue for a symmetric standard, then give the strongest objection to it and answer that objection.

## E. One week later (about 10 minutes)

**E1.** *NAI.* Without notes, name the three corners and the failure mode you think your institution is most
exposed to. Then name one cheap check from Section 14.3 that a department could run.

## What a strong answer looks like: C1

Two condensed interpretation paragraphs from a class of twelve. The class prompt asked for project ideas using
public data about their city.

**A weak response**

> The AI's ideas were a lot like each other and ours were more creative. This proves AI makes everyone think the
> same, so AI should not be allowed for project proposals.

**A strong response**

> *Counting rule, fixed before counting:* two ideas are the same if they would use the same data set to answer the
> same question.
>
> *Tally:* our 36 ideas contained 27 distinct ones. The AI's 36 contained 11. Nine of the twelve AI lists
> proposed transit ridership.
>
> *Interpretation:* in our class, the AI's lists overlapped much more than ours did, which fits the pattern in
> Section 14.3. Our sample is one class, one model, and one prompt, so it cannot say how large the effect is
> elsewhere. Giving everyone the same prompt also makes overlap more likely; varied prompts might have spread the
> AI's ideas further. The result is a reason to keep writing our own ideas first. It is not a reason to ban the
> tool: Section 14.3 suggests varying the starting point instead.

**What separates them**

- The weak response gives no count and no rule, so "more creative" cannot be checked.
- The strong response fixed its counting rule first, so the tally could not be tuned to the result.
- "Proves" overclaims. The strong response names three limits of its own sample, including one its design
  introduced.
- The strong response ends where the chapter does: with a design change, not a ban.

## For instructors

- **Minimum week:** A1 to A4, B1, and C1, then E1 a week later. About two hours of student time.
- **C1 is the anchor.** Collect the students' own ideas before anyone opens a tool, so step 1 stays uncontaminated.
  Write the shared prompt together, in class.
- **B1 may find no policy.** Treat that as a result. Students who report where they looked have done the exercise.
- **B2, D1, and D2 are about structures.** Keep the discussion away from named departments, colleagues, or
  administrators.
- **Privacy:** the pooled ideas in C1 can be anonymous. No student work goes into an AI tool in this set.
- **Looking ahead:** the [Chapter 15 set](ch15-the-framework-and-vocabulary.md) asks students to read their
  courses through the CAT framework and the four categories at the institution's scale.
