# Exercises for Chapter 1: Getting Started

*Part I, the student guide. Section numbers refer to v6.1.0 of the book. See [how the exercises are built](../exercise-design.md).*

*Each exercise starts with its assignment category from Section 3.4: NAI (No AI), AIT (AI as Tutor), AIC (AI as Collaborator), or AIS (AI as Object of Study). If your instructor sets a different category, follow theirs.*

## Key points

| | Key point | Section | Exercises |
|---|---|---|---|
| K1 | AI may help you learn, but it cannot learn for you. A correct-looking answer is not understanding, and your own sense of how much you learned can mislead you. | 1.1 | A1, D2, E1 |
| K2 | You can lean on a calculator; you cannot lean on AI until you have checked it. The value you add is judging whether an answer is right. | 1.1 | A1, C1, D2 |
| K3 | Two things come before asking AI anything: configure it, and make an honest attempt yourself. Skipping the attempt is the AI-first trap. | 1.2 | A4, B1, C1 |
| K4 | Configuration changes how the AI responds, not what it knows. Checking stands in for the answer key you do not have. | 1.2 | A2, C1 |
| K5 | Using AI well takes two different skills: tool-use skill and learning-with-AI skill. The pressure to accept help runs against the second. | 1.3 | A3, C1, C2 |
| K6 | A mentor prompt sets up the kind of help you get across many tasks, and adjusts as you go. | 1.3 | D1 |
| K7 | Keep the tutor inside your course: give it a map and a pin. A scope statement reduces the problem; it does not fence it. | 1.3 | B2, E2 |

## A. From memory (about 10 minutes)

Answer without notes. Then check each answer in the section the key-points table gives for it, and mark what you missed.

**A1.** *NAI.* Why can you lean on a calculator but not on an AI? Give one consequence for how you study this term.

**A2.** *NAI.* The chapter says configuration "changes how the AI responds, not what it knows." Explain the difference
in two sentences, and say what you still have to do because of it.

**A3.** *NAI.* Name the two skills from Section 1.3. Describe a student who is strong in one and weak in the other.

**A4.** *NAI.* What is the AI-first trap? What does "attempt first" require of you, and what does it not require?

## B. In your own courses

**B1. Your configuration.** *NAI. About 20 minutes.* For one course you are taking, write configuration
categories 1, 2, 3, and 5 from Section 1.2, in 150 words or fewer. Mark the line that most protects your
learning and the line that sets your integrity boundary. Save it in your AI's settings.
*Submit:* the configuration with the two lines marked.
*Graded on:* whether each category is specific to you and the course, not generic.

**B2. Map and pin.** *NAI. About 15 minutes.* For the same course, write a scope statement like the one in Section
1.3: what the course has covered (the map) and where you are now (the pin). Then name one method from later in
the course that an AI might reach for, and say why it would mislead you now.
*Submit:* the scope statement and your example.
*Graded on:* whether the pin draws a usable boundary, and whether the example explains the harm.

## C. With AI

**C1. Same question, two setups.** *AIT. About 45 minutes.*

1. Choose a concept from a course you are taking that you only half understand.
2. Before using any AI, write your own explanation of it in three sentences. Then write one sentence on what a
   good explanation would add.
3. Ask a fresh AI, with no configuration, to explain the concept. Save the reply.
4. Use your configuration from B1, plus the Study Partner Protocol
   ([`companion/spp/`](../../companion/spp/)), and ask the same question. Save the reply.
5. Pick one claim from either reply and check it against your textbook, notes, or instructor. Record what you
   found.
6. Rewrite your three-sentence explanation.

Then answer, in half a page:

- Which reply helped you learn, and what specifically did it do differently?
- Did either reply use a method your course has not reached?
- Which of the two skills from Section 1.3 did each step of this exercise use?

*Submit:* your first explanation, links to both replies, the claim you checked and how, your rewritten
explanation, and your half page. Add a one-line disclosure.
*Graded on:* the comparison between your two explanations and the claim you checked. The quality of the AI's
replies does not count.

**C2. Catch the offer.** *AIT. During a real study session this week.*

Section 1.3 warns that the hard moment is not when you ask the AI to do your work. It is when you have started
thinking and the AI offers to finish the problem for you. In one real study session, notice when that offer
comes. Record what the AI offered, what you did, and one line you could add to your setup so the offer comes
later or not at all.
*Submit:* the relevant part of the conversation (a shared link or a short excerpt) and three or four sentences.
Add a one-line disclosure.
*Graded on:* whether you named the moment accurately and whether your added line would change it.

## D. Going further ★

**D1. A mentor prompt.** *AIT. Two weeks.* Write a mentor prompt that does the four things Section 1.3
describes: placement on two separate axes, a placement you can see and correct, check-ins, and an auto-adjust
option. Use it for two weeks of real coursework. Report one time it changed the level of help, and whether it
was right to. Add a one-line disclosure.

**D2. Does the gap grow?** *NAI. One page.* Chapter 1 argues that as AI improves, a fluent answer gets cheaper
while judging one does not, so the gap between how polished work looks and what you understand grows wider.
Argue for or against this claim, using one example from your own field.

## E. One week later (5 minutes)

**E1.** *NAI.* Without notes, explain the concept you chose for C1 in five sentences. Compare with the explanation you
rewrote a week ago. What stayed, and what went?

**E2.** *NAI.* Write the scope statement for B2 again from memory, updated for where your course is now.

## What a strong answer looks like: C1

Two condensed responses to C1 from a student in a statistics course who chose the p-value. Both used the same
two AI setups. Only one did the exercise.

**A weak response**

> *My explanation:* A p-value tells you how likely your result is. Small means significant.
>
> *Comparison:* The configured reply was better because it was more personalized and asked me questions. The
> default reply was too long.
>
> *Claim checked:* I checked the definition and it was right.
>
> *Rewritten explanation:* The p-value is the probability, assuming the null hypothesis is true, of observing
> data at least as extreme as the data actually observed.

**A strong response**

> *My explanation:* A p-value is the chance that the null hypothesis is true. If it is below 0.05, the null is
> probably false. A good explanation would say where the 0.05 comes from.
>
> *Comparison:* The default reply gave the textbook definition in its first sentence and then listed five
> misconceptions. I read it and felt I understood. The configured reply asked me what "the chance the null is
> true" would even mean if the null is a fixed fact about the world. I could not answer, and that was the point
> where I saw my explanation was wrong. Neither reply used anything beyond our course, though the default one
> mentioned Bayes factors, which we have not covered.
>
> *Claim checked:* The default reply said p < 0.05 means "a 95% chance the result is real." Our textbook
> (Section 9.3) says nothing like that, and its definition conditions on the null being true. The reply was wrong
> in a way that matched my own mistake.
>
> *Rewritten explanation:* A p-value assumes the null hypothesis is true and asks how often data at least as
> extreme as ours would turn up. It is not the chance the null is true. A small p-value says the data would be
> surprising if the null held. The 0.05 cutoff is a convention, not a law.
>
> *Skills:* Writing my first explanation and checking the claim used learning-with-AI skill. Setting up the
> configuration used tool-use skill.

**What separates them**

- The weak first explanation is too vague to be wrong, so there is nothing to correct. The strong one commits to
  a claim, which is what makes the later correction possible.
- The weak comparison could be written without reading either reply. The strong one names the moment the
  configured reply changed the student's thinking, and admits that the default reply *felt* like understanding.
- The weak claim check names no source and no claim. The strong one quotes the claim, cites where it was
  checked, and finds a real error.
- The weak rewrite is the textbook definition, copied. It is correct and shows nothing. The strong rewrite is
  in the student's own words and fixes the specific mistake from the first attempt.

## For instructors

- **Minimum week:** A1 to A4, B1, and C1, then E1 a week later. About ninety minutes of student time.
- **C1 is the anchor.** Its graded product is the student's own explanation, before and after, and a checked
  claim. An AI can write the half page but cannot do the before and after for the student.
- **Expect varied results in C1.** Some students will find the unconfigured reply more useful. That is a good
  discussion, not a failed exercise: ask what the student was trying to do at that moment.
- **Privacy:** no exercise needs anything uploaded. B2 asks for a written scope, not a syllabus file.
- **Looking ahead:** attempting first (K3) returns in Chapter 3 with the Learning Spiral, and checking (K4)
  is the subject of Chapter 4. Later sets reuse the configuration from B1 and the scope from B2.
