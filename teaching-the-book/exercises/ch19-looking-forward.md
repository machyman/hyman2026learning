# Exercises for Chapter 19: Looking Forward

*Part III, the departmental and institutional guide, read from the student's side of the desk. Section numbers
refer to v6.1.1 of the book. See [how the exercises are built](../exercise-design.md). The book ends by saying
what it does not know. Several of its open questions can be answered from a student's seat, and this set asks you
to start.*

*Each exercise starts with its assignment category from Section 3.4: NAI (No AI), AIT (AI as Tutor), AIC (AI as Collaborator), or AIS (AI as Object of Study). If your instructor sets a different category, follow theirs.*

## Key points

| | Key point | Section | Exercises |
|---|---|---|---|
| K1 | Likely to change: AI grows more capable, tools become built into ordinary software, detectors grow less reliable, and students arrive more familiar with AI. Disclosure and verification generalize better than tool-by-tool rules. | 19.1.1 | A1, D1 |
| K2 | Likely to stay: the need for independent competence, the power of oral questioning while the student answers unaided, the signal in process evidence, and the growing weight of disclosure and verification. The competence costume gets cheaper every year; the competence it imitates does not. | 19.1.2 | A1, B3 |
| K3 | Two levers change the slope: no-AI assessment, which makes the learning loss visible, and integrity standards set centrally, so no single course pays alone for holding the line. | 19.1.4 | A3 |
| K4 | The evidence table separates what is established from what is untested. Whether courses built on this framework produce better learning is untested; no study has asked. A department that measures becomes that study. | 19.2 | A2, B1 |
| K5 | One cheap question: do students and instructors read the categories the same way? As written, NAI does not say whose AI use it forbids. | 19.2 | B1 |
| K6 | How students do without AI, on material they were taught with it, is the measure that speaks most to learning. | 19.2.2 | B3 |
| K7 | The risk register includes false confidence: fluent AI output leads students to overestimate what they understand, and self-assessment does not track the deficit. | 19.3 | B2, B3 |
| K8 | The model policy is meant to be short enough that students can give it to the AI tools they use. | 19.4 | C1 |
| K9 | The performance-versus-learning gap: AI can raise the apparent quality of work without improving learning. Judging policy by submitted work misses it; judging it by independent performance after AI use catches it. | 19.5 | A4 |

## A. From memory (about 10 minutes)

Answer without notes. Then check each answer in the section the key-points table gives for it, and mark what you missed.

**A1.** *NAI.* Name two things Section 19.1 expects to change and two it expects to stay the same.

**A2.** *NAI.* Which claims in the evidence table are untested? What would change their status?

**A3.** *NAI.* Name the two levers in Section 19.1.4. What happens without them?

**A4.** *NAI.* What is the performance-versus-learning gap? Name two kinds of evidence that would catch it.

## B. In your own courses

**B1. Do we read the labels the same way?** *NAI. About 15 minutes, as a class.* Section 19.2 asks whether students
and instructors read the categories alike. Answer three questions privately and anonymously. Does an NAI label
forbid only your AI use, or the instructor's too? Under AIT, may an AI check your finished answer? Under AIC, are
you graded against different expectations than under AIT? Then pool the answers and compare them with your
instructor's.
*Submit:* your own answers, then the class tally and one sentence on the largest disagreement.
*Graded on:* completion. There are no wrong answers in the private round.

**B2. Your risk register.** *NAI. About 20 minutes.* From the risk register in Section 19.3, choose three risks you
have seen from the student's side. For each, describe what you saw without naming anyone, and say whether the
recommended response is in place in your courses.
*Submit:* a three-row table.
*Graded on:* whether each row names something observed, not something assumed.

**B3. Predict, then check.** *NAI. About 30 minutes.* Choose a topic you studied with AI help this term. First,
predict on a 1 to 5 scale how well you could solve a new problem on it without AI. Then find or write one transfer
problem, a nearby case you have not seen, and solve it with no tools and no notes. Check your answer against a
source you trust.
*Submit:* your prediction, your attempt, the check, and two sentences on the gap between prediction and result.
*Graded on:* the honesty of the comparison, not on the score.

## C. With AI

**C1. Give the rule to the tool.** *AIS. About 50 minutes, on a practice problem only.*

Section 19.4 suggests publishing a policy short enough that students can give it to their AI tools. This exercise
tests what that achieves.

1. Write a standing instruction of three to five sentences from the AIT definition in Section 15.2.2, or from your
   own course's AIT wording. Give it to an AI at the start of a fresh conversation.
2. Choose an ungraded practice problem. Make five requests that drift toward production: ask for an explanation,
   ask it to check your answer, ask for the first line, ask what a strong answer would include, and ask for the
   solution outright. Record what it gives you each time.
3. Revise the instruction once to close the gaps you found, and run the same five requests in a new conversation.
4. Say what the instruction achieved, and what still depends on you.

*Submit:* both instructions, a table of the ten responses (or shared links), your conclusion, the tool and the
date, and a one-line disclosure.
*Graded on:* whether the table shows where the line held and where it slid, and whether the conclusion says who
holds the line when the tool does not.

## D. Going further ★

**D1. Sealed predictions.** *NAI. One page.* Write three dated predictions about how AI will change coursework in
your field by the time you graduate. For each, say which of the framework's structural commitments (Section
19.1.3) would survive it, and which details would need to change. Keep the page. Reread it in your final term.

**D2. Tutor or mentor?** *NAI. One page.* Section 19.2 asks whether the difference between a tutor and a mentor is
large enough to need its own category, or whether it is a matter of relationship that cuts across all four. Answer
from your own experience of human tutors, mentors, and AI tools. Then give the strongest case for the other answer.

## E. One week later (about 10 minutes)

**E1.** *NAI.* Without notes, name the two levers and the measure that speaks most to learning. Then compare your
B3 prediction with your result. Would you predict differently now?

## What a strong answer looks like: C1

Two condensed conclusions from step 4.

**A weak response**

> The AI followed the rules well. When I asked for the answer, it refused and gave me a hint instead. So giving the
> AI the policy works.

**A strong response**

> | Request | First instruction | Revised instruction |
> |---|---|---|
> | Explain the idea | Explained | Explained |
> | Check my answer | Rewrote my solution in full | Said where the error was, nothing more |
> | Write the first line | Wrote the setup, which was the main step | Declined, gave a hint |
> | What would a strong answer include? | Listed the steps of the solution | Listed the steps of the solution |
> | Give me the solution | Declined | Declined |
>
> *Conclusion:* the first instruction held only against the direct request. The revision ("never write any line of
> the solution; when asked to check, say only whether and where an error is") held on four of five. It still slid on
> "what would a strong answer include," which produced the solution as a list. The instruction helps, but it does
> not hold the line. What holds it is a check I run: close the tool and rebuild the solution from a blank page
> (Section 11.2.1). One model, one problem, one day; another tool may fail differently.

**What separates them**

- The weak response tested only the direct request, the one a tool is most likely to refuse.
- The strong response tested the gradual crossing, where Section 11.2.1 says a written rule cannot catch it.
- The strong response revised once and reports what still failed, rather than stopping at success.
- It ends with the check that does not depend on the tool, and with the limits of its own test.

## For instructors

- **Minimum week:** A1 to A4, B1, B3, and C1, then E1 a week later. About two hours of student time.
- **C1 is the anchor.** Use an ungraded practice problem. The point is that guardrails leak, so the student's own
  check matters; it is not a contest to break the tool.
- **B1 is a small piece of the study Section 19.2 says is missing.** Keep the private round anonymous. If the
  class agrees, share the tally with your department.
- **B3 grades honesty, not the score.** A large gap between prediction and result is the finding.
- **Last chapter.** The [Appendix A set](appA-ethical-scientific-writing.md) on ethical AI-assisted scientific
  writing can follow for students who write research.
