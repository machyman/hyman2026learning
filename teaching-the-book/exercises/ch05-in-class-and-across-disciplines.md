# Exercises for Chapter 5: In Class and Across Disciplines

*Part I, the student guide. Section numbers refer to v6.1.0 of the book. See [how the exercises are built](../exercise-design.md).*

*Each exercise starts with its assignment category from Section 3.4: NAI (No AI), AIT (AI as Tutor), AIC (AI as Collaborator), or AIS (AI as Object of Study). If your instructor sets a different category, follow theirs.*

## Key points

| | Key point | Section | Exercises |
|---|---|---|---|
| K1 | "If you submitted it, you should be able to explain it." What you defend depends on your field. Class size, not course level, sets the model. | 5.1 | A1, B1, C2, E1 |
| K2 | To prepare, ask AI to play a skeptical instructor and find the weakest part of your explanation. Then practice answering without looking at the AI response. | 5.1 | C2, E1 |
| K3 | Large classes ask for evidence of thinking: records of how you reasoned, revised, verified, explained, or made choices. The homework-to-quiz check and the audit-and-repair task are two common forms. | 5.2 | A2, B2, D1, E1 |
| K4 | Some courses put most of the grade on in-person, closed-book work. That removes the incentive to hide AI use, at the cost of a few high-stakes moments. Hybrid courses keep the same expectations: explain what you submitted, verify what you used, own the work. | 5.2 | B2, D2 |
| K5 | A useful AI question keeps the central thinking with you. In computational mathematics, ask AI to check, debug, and probe; in proof-based work, to critique, generate examples, and identify gaps. In related STEM fields, the stronger prompt keeps the field-specific judgment with you. | 5.3 | B3, C1, E2 |
| K6 | AI tools are study-technique amplifiers. The prompt decides whether AI amplifies a strong technique, such as practice testing or self-explanation, or a weak one, such as rereading or summarizing. | 5.3 | A3, C1, E2 |
| K7 | Outside the quantitative fields, good questions and real verification differ by field. Move first, then ask AI to challenge you. Verify by returning to the source, running the check, or checking against the source of authority. Watch for fluent paraphrase, borrowed certainty, and the confident wrong answer. | 5.4 | A4, B3, C1 |
| K8 | Each vignette runs the whole Learning Spiral. AI gives one narrow kind of help. The student does the work, checks it against something other than the AI, and discloses. Your instructor's policy always comes first. | 5.5 | C1, D3 |

## A. From memory (about 10 minutes)

Answer without notes. Then check each answer in the section the key-points table gives for it, and mark what you missed.

**A1.** *NAI.* What is the one standard behind the Oral-Defense Model? What decides whether a course uses that model or
the Evidence-of-Thinking Model, and what does not?

**A2.** *NAI.* Name four kinds of evidence of thinking a large course might ask for. What does the homework-to-quiz check
test?

**A3.** *NAI.* Name two study techniques with strong learning value and two with limited value. Why do weak prompts tend
to amplify the limited ones?

**A4.** *NAI.* For interpretive fields, empirical social science, and professional fields, name the verification move and
the failure mode from Section 5.4. Which pair fits your own field best?

## B. In your own courses

**B1. Your defense card.** *NAI. About 20 minutes.* Choose one piece of work you have submitted this term. Find your
field in the list in Section 5.1. Write the three questions an instructor in that field would most likely ask about
the work. Answer each from memory in two or three sentences. Mark the answer you are least sure of.
*Submit:* the questions, your answers, and the marked answer.
*Graded on:* whether the questions ask what your field expects you to defend, and whether your answers explain
choices rather than restate the work.

**B2. Read your course.** *NAI. About 15 minutes.* For one course you are taking, list the evidence-of-thinking
practices from Section 5.2 that it uses, and the share of the grade that rests on in-person, closed-book work.
Which model is it closest to: Oral-Defense, Evidence-of-Thinking, or Hybrid? Name the check most likely to expose a
gap in your understanding now, and one thing you will do this week to close it.
*Submit:* your answers, in 150 words or fewer.
*Graded on:* whether your reading matches the syllabus, and whether the gap is specific enough to act on.

**B3. Three stronger prompts.** *NAI. About 15 minutes.* For a course you are taking, write three pairs of prompts, each
a weak one and a stronger one. Model them on Section 5.3 (mathematics, physics, computer science, statistics) or
Section 5.4 (interpretive, social science, professional fields). For each stronger prompt, name the judgment it
keeps with you and how you would verify the reply.
*Submit:* the three pairs, each with its judgment and verification move.
*Graded on:* whether each stronger prompt leaves a real decision with you, rather than asking more politely for the
same answer.

## C. With AI

**C1. Weak prompt, stronger prompt.** *AIT. About 45 minutes.*

1. Choose a problem, reading, or case from a course that allows AI on it. No restricted exam questions and nothing
   private (Section 4.6).
2. Before using any AI, make your first move. In a STEM field, write your setup or proof attempt and mark where you
   are stuck. In an interpretive field, write your reading in one sentence and name the passage it rests on. In
   social science, write your question, data, and candidate explanation. In a professional field, reason through
   the case and name the governing standard.
3. Predict which study technique a weak prompt would amplify here, and which a stronger one would.
4. Use your configuration from [Chapter 1](ch01-getting-started.md) (exercise B1) or the Study Partner Protocol
   ([`companion/spp/`](../../companion/spp/)). Send a stronger prompt of your field's kind, perhaps one from B3.
   Finish the work yourself.
5. Verify with your field's move. Record what you checked, against what, and what you found.
6. Only now, in a fresh chat without your configuration, send the weak version. Ask the AI to solve the problem,
   supply the interpretation, draw the conclusion, or act as the authority. Save the reply. Running it last keeps
   it from replacing your attempt.

Then answer, in half a page:

- Which technique did each prompt amplify? Was your prediction right?
- What judgment did the stronger prompt leave with you? Did the weak reply make it for you, and was it right?
- If your field is in Section 5.4, did the weak reply show that field's failure mode?

*Submit:* your first move and prediction, both prompts with links to both chats, your verification record, and your
half page. Add a one-line disclosure.
*Graded on:* your first move, your verification record, and the comparison. The quality of the AI's replies does
not count.

**C2. A mock defense.** *AIT. About 30 minutes, over two days.*

Section 5.1 suggests using AI to prepare for questioning, then answering without looking at the AI. Use the work and
the marked answer from B1.

1. In a fresh chat, ask the AI to play a skeptical instructor in your field. Tell it to ask one question at a time,
   to question your assumptions, and to find the weakest part of your explanation, but not to supply answers.
   Section 4.1.6 has prompts you can adapt.
2. Type a short explanation of the work in your own words. Do not paste the submitted work, a classmate's work, or
   restricted course material.
3. Answer five of its questions. Copy the questions out.
4. The next day, answer them again in writing, without the chat or your notes.
5. Did the AI find the answer you marked in B1? Did it find a weak spot you missed?

*Submit:* the five questions, your answers from step 4, and three or four sentences comparing them with B1. Add a
one-line disclosure.
*Graded on:* whether your step 4 answers stand without help, and whether your comparison is honest about what you
missed.

## D. Going further ★

**D1. Audit and repair.** *AIS. About 40 minutes.* Section 5.2 says this task "rewards judgment
over fluency." Pick a question from a course you are taking, on material you have studied. Ask a fresh AI for a
full answer that hides one subtle error, and tell it not to say where. Find the error, explain why it matters, and
repair it, checking against your textbook or notes. Then ask the AI where the error was. If its account differs
from yours, check both against your course materials: the AI is what you are studying, not the key. The grade rests
on your audit and the source you checked. Add a one-line disclosure.

**D2. Where should the grade rest?** *NAI. One page.* Section 5.2 says that putting the grade's weight on
in-person, closed-book assessment "removes the incentive to hide AI use rather than trying to detect it," but suits
some students less well than others. From where you sit, argue whether one of your courses should move that way.
Who would it help, who would it hurt, and how would your own study habits change?

**D3. Your field's vignette.** *AIT. One week.* Write a vignette like those in Section 5.5 from a real week
of your own coursework, on a task where your course allows AI. Use the five stages: Try to solve, Ask, Work, Verify,
Reflect and disclose. Keep it under 200 words, and end with one sentence on where the reasoning stayed. The grade
rests on whether each stage shows what you made, and whether Verify checks against something other than the AI.
Add a one-line disclosure.

## E. One week later (5 minutes)

**E1.** *NAI.* Run a homework-to-quiz check on yourself. Without notes, answer the C2 question you found hardest. Compare
with your answer from a week ago. What held, and what went?

**E2.** *NAI.* From memory, write the stronger prompt you used in C1, the technique it amplified, and how you verified the
reply. Then write one stronger prompt for something you are studying this week.

## What a strong answer looks like: C1

Two condensed responses to C1 from a student in an introductory philosophy course, working on Descartes's Second
Meditation. Both used the same setup. Only one did the exercise.

**A weak response**

> *First move:* Descartes says "I think, therefore I am." I need to explain what that means.
>
> *Prediction:* The stronger prompt will be better for learning.
>
> *Stronger prompt:* "Explain the cogito in simple terms, with an example."
>
> *Verification:* I compared the AI's explanation with a study-guide website. They agreed.
>
> *Comparison:* The weak prompt ("What is Descartes's argument in the Second Meditation?") gave the clearer reply,
> so I would use it next time. The stronger prompt used elaborative interrogation.

**A strong response**

> *First move:* Descartes doubts all he can, even that he has a body, and finds one thing he cannot doubt while
> doubting: that he exists. The passage is where he concludes that "I am, I exist" is necessarily true whenever he
> puts it forward or conceives it. I am least sure whether he means this as an argument with premises.
>
> *Prediction:* The weak prompt would amplify summarizing. Mine would amplify elaborative interrogation.
>
> *Stronger prompt:* "Here is my reading of the Second Meditation: [my two sentences]. Give me the strongest
> objection to it and a passage that complicates it. Do not give me your own reading."
>
> *Work and verification:* The AI said an argument from "I think" to "I exist" needs a hidden premise: whatever
> thinks exists. I returned to the paragraph in our assigned translation. It states no such premise. The certainty
> comes each time he thinks the proposition. I revised my thesis to say so.
>
> *Weak prompt, run last:* "What is Descartes's argument in the Second Meditation?" The smooth reply quoted "I
> think, therefore I am" as the Meditation's own words. Our translation has no such sentence. That wording comes
> from the *Discourse on the Method*.
>
> *Comparison:* My prediction was half right. The weak reply did invite summarizing: it felt complete. The stronger
> prompt worked more like self-explanation, since answering the objection made me connect the passage to what I
> already thought. It left me the judgment of what the passage supports. The weak reply made that judgment and
> misquoted the text. That is fluent paraphrase.

**What separates them**

- The weak first move quotes a famous line and stops, so the AI has no reading to argue against. The strong one
  commits to a reading, names its passage, and marks its doubt.
- The weak "stronger" prompt still asks the AI for the interpretation, where Section 5.4.1 says AI is least safe.
  The strong prompt asks for an objection and a passage, and rules out the AI's own reading.
- The weak verification checks one summary against another. Two summaries agreeing is not evidence. The strong one
  returns to the source and finds the famous line is not in the assigned text.
- The weak comparison prefers the clearer reply and names a technique with no evidence. The strong one names the
  failure mode and admits its prediction was half wrong.

## For instructors

- **Minimum week:** A1 to A4, B3, and C1, then E2 a week later. About seventy-five minutes of student time. In a
  course with oral defenses, add B1 and C2, then E1.
- **C1 is the anchor.** Its graded product is the first move, the verification record, and the comparison. An AI
  can write the half page but cannot move first or go back to the source for the student.
- **E1 is a homework-to-quiz check.** Say so. Students then meet the Evidence-of-Thinking Model from the inside.
- **Other fields:** students outside the fields of Sections 5.3 and 5.4 use the closest section and say what
  differs. Creative work is in Section 5.1's defense list but not in 5.3 or 5.4. For C1, those students can follow
  Section 3.3: their own ideas first, then alternatives with no verdict. The
  [study sessions](../../students/study-sessions/) model stronger prompts in four STEM fields.
- **Privacy:** C2 uses a fresh explanation, not the submitted work. D1 uses only AI-written answers. No exercise
  needs anything uploaded.
- **Looking ahead:** Chapter 10 treats the three assessment models from the instructor's side, and Section 11.4
  adds discipline overlays. Students who did B2 and D2 will read them with a case in hand. Appendix B collects the
  mathematical patterns from Parts I and II.
