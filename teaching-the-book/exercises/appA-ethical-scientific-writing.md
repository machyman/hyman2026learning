# Exercises for Appendix A: Best Practices for Ethical AI-Assisted Scientific Writing

*Appendix A, for graduate students and undergraduates doing research. Section numbers refer to v6.1.0 of the
book. See [how the exercises are built](../exercise-design.md).*

*Each exercise starts with its assignment category from Section 3.4: NAI (No AI), AIT (AI as Tutor), AIC (AI as Collaborator), or AIS (AI as Object of Study). If your instructor sets a different category, follow theirs.*

## Key points

| | Key point | Section | Exercises |
|---|---|---|---|
| K1 | Authors can divide the work of understanding, not the duty to answer for the whole paper. Every essential part needs an author who can defend it. If the only answer to a reviewer is what the tool suggested, the work is not ready. | A.1 | A1, B1, D3 |
| K2 | AI can produce research-level results but cannot answer for them, so it cannot be an author. A formal proof does not show that the formalization captures the intended problem, or that the result matters. | A.1 | A2, D2, D3 |
| K3 | What goes into a tool matters as much as what comes out. Keep anything not already public in institutional or closed environments. Never run a manuscript you are reviewing through an external tool. | A.1 | A4, C1 |
| K4 | The literature runs on a reserve of trust. Submitting AI output you have not verified "converts a private convenience into a public cost." | A.1 | A4, C1 |
| K5 | Verify all AI-assisted content yourself. Reading well is not being correct, and being correct is not saying what you meant (workflow steps 2, 3, and 4). The tools are stochastic, so check that a result reproduces. | A.2 | A3, C2, D1 |
| K6 | A finder's posted result "is a claim, not yet a result." The finder searches the literature, finds a qualified partner, states what was checked, and discloses the tools. Credit follows contribution and responsibility. | A.2 | A2, D2 |
| K7 | Disclosure matches involvement. Under-disclosure is unethical, and over-disclosure can misstate your contribution the other way. Using AI as a method is substantial use. A capstone or thesis adds a Responsibility field to the student block. | A.1, A.3 | B2, C1 |
| K8 | The standard is the ordinary one; AI makes a lapse harder to see. Four pitfalls share one gap, between what smooth text claims and what you checked, and one remedy. For citations, the standard is reading the source, not confirming that it exists. | A.1, A.4, A.6 | C1, C2, D1, E2 |

## A. From memory (about 10 minutes)

Answer without notes. Then check each answer in the section the key-points table gives for it, and mark what you missed.

**A1.** *NAI.* On a multi-author paper, what can the authors divide among themselves, and what can they not? What
makes a part of the work "essential"? Give one kind of essential part.

**A2.** *NAI.* Section A.2 says a result posted by a finder from outside the field "is a claim, not yet a result."
Why? Name the four things the finder can do. Who may be listed as an author, and who may not?

**A3.** *NAI.* Name the five steps of the verification workflow. Why is a passage that reads well not yet verified?
Why is a correct passage not yet finished?

**A4.** *NAI.* Give two reasons, from Section A.1, not to run a manuscript you are reviewing through an external AI
tool. Then name one other way AI strains the trust that peer review runs on.

## B. In your own research

Use a project you are on: a thesis, a lab project, a poster, a paper in preparation. If you are not on one
yet, use a published paper in your field. Describe the work in your own words; do not hand in your group's
unpublished data or drafts.

**B1. Who answers for what.** *NAI. About 25 minutes.* For your project, or a published paper with an
author-contribution statement, list three to five essential parts. Use the three kinds in Section A.1: a
result other steps depend on, a computation no later step would expose as wrong, an interpretive claim the
conclusion rests on. Name who answers for each (a role is enough), and mark the parts you own. For one of
them, write the answer you would give a reviewer who asks why that approach was chosen: three sentences, no
mention of a tool. Add one sentence on where your own understanding ends.
*Submit:* the list with owners marked, your reviewer answer, and your sentence.
*Graded on:* whether the parts are ones the argument turns on, and whether the answer rests on understanding
rather than on an account of what was done.

**B2. The right level of disclosure.** *NAI. About 20 minutes.* List every way you have used AI on your project,
or plan to in the next month. Sort each use as minor, moderate, substantial, or central (Section A.1). Then
write the disclosure the work needs. For a manuscript, adapt a template from Section A.3 and say where each
part goes. For a capstone or thesis, use the six-field student block
([`students/disclosure-templates.md`](../../students/disclosure-templates.md)) plus the Responsibility field
from Section A.3. Mark the line a careless writer would leave out, and the line that would claim too much.
Keep the drafts and logs behind it; Section A.3 explains why they protect writers whom AI-text detectors
misjudge.
*Submit:* the sorted list and the disclosure, with the two lines marked.
*Graded on:* whether the level matches the uses, and whether a reader could tell what the AI did and who
checked it.

## C. With AI

**C1. Check every citation.** *AIS. About ninety minutes, most of it reading.*

Section A.4 sets the standard for citations: "reading the source, not confirming that it exists." This
exercise applies it to your own field.

1. Choose a research area you know a little: your thesis topic, your group's area, or the area of a paper you
   are reading. Keep it narrow. "Groundwater recharge in karst aquifers" works; "hydrology" does not.
2. Before using any AI, write from memory three to five key findings of recent work in the area, one sentence
   each. Mark each *sure*, *fairly sure*, or *guessing*. Predict how many of five references will fail each
   check in step 4.
3. Ask an AI for a one-page summary of recent work in the area, with five references. Describe the area in
   public terms only; paste in no draft, data, or unpublished work (Section A.1). Save the reply as a shared
   link ([`students/sharing-ai-conversations.md`](../../students/sharing-ai-conversations.md)).
4. Check every reference in a table with three columns:
   - **Exists.** Find it in a record your field trusts. Section 4.4.6 names MathSciNet, Web of Science, the
     publisher's record, and the journal page.
   - **Details right.** Authors, year, title, venue, DOI.
   - **Says what the summary claims.** Open the paper and read the part behind the claim. Record what it
     actually says, and where.
5. Rewrite the summary using only what you confirmed. Drop any claim whose source failed. Where a source
   hedges, hedge too. End with a one-line disclosure.

Then answer, in half a page:

- Which of your beliefs from step 2 did the check confirm, which did it change, and which did it leave
  untested?
- Which column caught the most failures, and how did that compare with your prediction?
- Did the summary's errors lean one way, toward saying more or less than the sources?

*Submit:* your step 2 list and predictions, the link to the AI reply, the table, your rewritten summary with
its disclosure, and your half page.
*Graded on:* the third column above all. Each "yes" there needs what the paper says and where; a "yes"
without that earns nothing. Then the rewrite and the half page. The quality of the AI's summary does not
count, and neither does the number of fake references you find.

**C2. Suggests or shows.** *AIS. About 40 minutes.*

Section A.4 warns that AI revision can turn a result that "suggests" a conclusion into one that "shows" or
"proves" it.

1. Pick a published paper in your field that you have read. Write a 150-word paragraph, in your own words, on
   its main result. Hedge where the paper hedges. Write it fresh, not from an unpublished draft.
2. Underline every word that sets how certain or how general a claim is: *suggests*, *shows*, *may*, *in this
   sample*. Predict how many an AI edit will change.
3. Ask an AI to revise the paragraph "for a high-impact journal." Save the reply.
4. List every change in certainty or scope, and every new claim. For each, check the paper: which version
   does it support?
5. Write the final paragraph yourself. Keep only the edits that keep your meaning.

*Submit:* both versions with the words marked, your list of changes, your final paragraph, and a one-line
disclosure.
*Graded on:* whether you caught every change in certainty or scope and checked each one against the paper.

## D. Going further ★

**D1. Run it, test it, run it again.** *AIC. About two hours.* Pick a computation in your
field with a known answer: an analytical solution, a published benchmark, or a small case you can work by
hand. Before asking any AI, write three known-input tests and two boundary cases. Ask an AI to write the
code, using only public information. Then ask again, with the same prompt, in a fresh session. Run your tests
on both versions. Report which tests each passed, where the versions differ, and what you would have reported
had you trusted the first run. The code and numerical rows of the table in Section A.6 are the standard. Add
a one-line disclosure.

**D2. Credit for the question.** *NAI. One to two pages.* Section A.2 says a finder who chose or framed the
right problem "can be an author without writing a line of the proof, if they approve the paper and can answer
for what was asked, how, and why." Argue for or against this rule, using a case from your own field, real or
plausible. Say what the finder's four steps would look like there.

**D3. When only a machine can check.** *NAI. One page.* Section A.2 ends by warning that the verification
cornerstone assumes a human can check what AI produces, and that this will not hold forever. Suppose one
essential part of a paper you co-author can be checked only by a machine. Write the disclosure paragraph you
would want. Say what you could still answer for, and what you could not.

## E. One week later (5 minutes)

**E1.** *NAI.* Without notes, write the key findings of your C1 area again. Compare them with your rewritten
summary. Which corrections stuck, and which slipped back to what you believed before?

**E2.** *NAI.* Name the four pitfalls in Section A.4 and the one habit that closes all of them. For each pitfall,
name the check you would run.

## What a strong answer looks like: C1

Two condensed responses to C1 from first-year graduate students in ecology who chose wild bee decline. Both
used the same AI and got five references. Only one did the exercise.

**A weak response**

> *From memory:* Bees are declining because of pesticides and climate change.
>
> *Table:* I looked up all five references on Google Scholar. Four exist. I could not find Ref. 4, so I
> removed it. Details right: yes for all four. Says what the summary claims: yes for all four.
>
> *Rewritten summary:* The AI's summary, with Ref. 4 and its sentence removed.
>
> *Disclosure:* I used AI to help with this summary.
>
> *Beliefs:* The check confirmed what I believed.

**A strong response**

> *From memory:* (1) Neonicotinoid pesticides are the main cause of wild bee decline. *Sure.* (2) Declines
> are better measured in honeybees than in wild bees. *Fairly sure.* (3) Flower strips on farms bring wild
> bees back. *Guessing.* Prediction: one reference will not exist, one will have wrong details, the rest will
> pass.
>
> *Table (four of five rows):*
>
> - *Ref. 2.* Exists; details right. Does not say what the summary claims. Summary: "showed that
>   neonicotinoids cause colony collapse." Paper: lower foraging in one lab study at one dose; the
>   Discussion calls the field effect uncertain.
> - *Ref. 3.* Exists. Details wrong: the year and journal belong to an earlier paper by the same group. The
>   claim fits that earlier paper, not this one.
> - *Ref. 4.* No record in Web of Science or the journal's archive.
> - *Ref. 5.* Exists; details right. Partly: farms with flower strips held more bees, but Section 4.2 says
>   the design cannot tell whether the strips grew populations or only drew bees in.
>
> In all, one reference did not exist, one had wrong details, and three of the four real ones did not fully
> say what the summary claimed. My prediction held for the first two columns and failed on the third.
>
> *Rewritten summary (excerpt):* A review I confirmed (Ref. 1) treats wild bee decline as having
> several causes that act together, among them habitat loss, pesticides, and disease. One lab study found
> that a neonicotinoid reduced foraging at one dose; its authors call the field effect uncertain (Ref. 2).
> Farms with flower strips hold more wild bees (Ref. 5), but whether the strips grow populations is open.
>
> *Disclosure:* I used [tool and version] to draft a summary with references, checked every reference, read
> the part of each source behind its claim, and wrote this version myself.
>
> *Beliefs:* Belief 1 changed: no source I kept calls pesticides the main cause. Belief 3 is now a question,
> not a finding. Belief 2 went untested; no source I kept measured it. Every claim that failed the third
> column said more than its paper, never less, and each leaned toward my belief 1. I would have read
> straight past them.

**What separates them**

- The weak response checks only existence, the easy column. Its "yes" in the third column cites nothing, so
  it shows no reading. The strong one records what each paper says, and where.
- The weak belief is too vague for any check to change. The strong beliefs commit and carry a confidence
  mark, so the check can confirm or overturn them.
- The weak rewrite is the AI's summary minus one sentence. It still carries every claim no one read. The
  strong rewrite says only what the sources say, with their hedges.
- The weak disclosure does not say what the tool did or who checked it. The strong one says both.
- The strong response notices which way the errors leaned: toward the student's own belief. Section A.4
  warns that the pull to accept is strongest when the output confirms a favored hypothesis.

## For instructors

- **Minimum week:** A1 to A4, B1, and C1, then E1 a week later. About two hours of student time, most of
  it reading in C1.
- **C1 is the anchor. Weight its third column most heavily.** Many students will find that every reference
  exists and stop there. Section 4.3.3 explains why that is not enough: a model tied to a scholarly index
  returns real references, and the failure moves downstream to what the source says. Some summaries will
  pass every check. Grade the record of checking and the beliefs it tested, not the number of errors found.
- **Two useful variants.** Split the class between a general assistant and a paper-grounded search tool (see
  [`students/undergraduate-research.md`](../../students/undergraduate-research.md)) and compare where each
  group's failures fall. Or run C1 with the configuration from the Chapter 1 set plus category 6, the
  technical-work accuracy guard, which Section A.2 singles out for research writing.
- **Privacy:** no exercise needs anything uploaded. C1 describes the area in public terms, C2 uses a
  paragraph written fresh about a published paper, and D1 uses public information. B1 and B2 ask students
  to describe their work, not to hand in the group's files. Never ask students to bring a manuscript they
  are reviewing (Section A.1).
- **Undergraduates and labels:** students new to research can start with
  [`students/undergraduate-research.md`](../../students/undergraduate-research.md). D1's label, AI as
  Collaborator, names a working role in an assignment; Section A.1 notes that it makes no claim about
  authorship.
- **Looking back:** this set takes Chapter 4 to research grade. Citations (4.3.3), meaning drift (4.3.4),
  code that runs (4.3.5), verification in graduate research (4.4.6), and disclosure (4.5) all return here.
  The AI use log (7.5) is the record behind B2's disclosure, and Section 7.7 sets up the research setting.
