# Exercises for Chapter 18: The Institution's Toolkit

*Part III, the departmental and institutional guide, read from the student's side of the desk. Section numbers
refer to v6.1.1 of the book. See [how the exercises are built](../exercise-design.md). Chapter 18 collects forms,
so this set is short. It uses the forms that touch you directly and checks your institution against the rest.*

*Each exercise starts with its assignment category from Section 3.4: NAI (No AI), AIT (AI as Tutor), AIC (AI as Collaborator), or AIS (AI as Object of Study). If your instructor sets a different category, follow theirs.*

## Key points

| | Key point | Section | Exercises |
|---|---|---|---|
| K1 | Two commitments should not be relaxed when the framework is adapted locally: the minimum independent-competence requirement and transparent disclosure. | 18.1 | A1 |
| K2 | The framework is a structure, not a guarantee, and it does not aim at uniformity. Programs will weight Core Competence, AI-assisted Practice, and Trustworthiness differently. | 18.2 | B2 |
| K3 | The syllabus template puts the student's side in one line: do not submit AI output you cannot explain, verify, or defend. | 18.3.2 | C1 |
| K4 | The privacy checklist asks eight questions before AI is used in a course, including whether students must create personal accounts, whether there is a no-cost option, and whether the tool is accessible. | 18.3.4 | A3, B1 |
| K5 | The integrity workflow starts by rereading the rule and the disclosure, names a specific concern, and invites the student to explain. It then decides among four cases: misunderstanding, poor documentation, prohibited assistance, or deliberate misrepresentation. | 18.3.5 | A2 |
| K6 | The five-level program rubric runs from Fragmented to Exemplary. An institution is rarely at the same level in every respect. | 18.3.7 | B2 |
| K7 | Ten commitments summarize the institutional framework on one page. | 18.3.8 | C1, D1 |

## A. From memory (about 10 minutes)

Answer without notes. Then check each answer in the section the key-points table gives for it, and mark what you missed.

**A1.** *NAI.* Which two commitments should not be relaxed when the framework is adapted locally?

**A2.** *NAI.* Name the four cases the integrity workflow distinguishes. Why does the distinction matter to a
student?

**A3.** *NAI.* Name five of the eight privacy-checklist questions. Which bear most directly on students?

## B. In your own courses

**B1. Check a tool you are asked to use.** *NAI. About 20 minutes.* Choose one AI tool a course asks you to use, or
one you use for coursework. Answer the eight questions in Section 18.3.4 from the student's side, using the tool's
terms and your institution's pages. Where you cannot find an answer, say where you looked.
*Submit:* an eight-row table with links.
*Graded on:* whether each answer traces to a page you can link.

**B2. Rate your department.** *NAI. About 20 minutes.* Using the rubric in Section 18.3.7, rate your department
separately on five elements: course AI policies, shared vocabulary, privacy rules, assessment models, and disclosure.
Base each rating on at least three syllabi.
*Submit:* five ratings, each with its evidence.
*Graded on:* whether the evidence comes from documents, not impressions.

## C. With AI

**C1. Read the policy, then check the AI.** *NAI, then AIS. About 50 minutes.*

1. **Your reading (NAI).** Read your institution's or department's published AI policy. Mark which of the ten
   commitments in Section 18.3.8 it makes, with a short quotation for each.
2. **The AI without the document (AIS).** Ask an AI what your institution's policy on student AI use is. Do not
   give it the document.
3. **The AI with the document (AIS).** Give it the published policy and ask it to map the policy to the ten
   commitments.
4. **Compare.** Check every claim in both answers against the policy's text. Classify each discrepancy as
   invented, omitted, outdated, or overstated.

*Submit:* your reading with quotations, both AI answers (or shared links), a discrepancy table, a paragraph on what
a student who asked the AI instead of reading the policy would get wrong, and a one-line disclosure.
*Graded on:* whether every discrepancy is checked against the policy's text and classified.

## D. Going further ★

**D1. The commitment that matters most.** *NAI. One page.* Choose the one commitment in Section 18.3.8 whose absence
would harm students most. Argue for it from Part III, then give the strongest case for a different choice and
answer it.

## E. One week later (about 10 minutes)

**E1.** *NAI.* Without notes, write as many of the ten commitments as you can. Name one your institution's policy
makes and one it does not.

## What a strong answer looks like: C1

Two condensed answers to step 4.

**A weak response**

> The AI was pretty accurate. It said our university allows AI with instructor permission and requires
> disclosure, which matches what I read.

**A strong response**

> | AI's claim | Policy text | Class |
> |---|---|---|
> | Without the document: the university screens submissions with AI-detection software. | Says nothing about screening; says detector output cannot be the sole basis of a finding. | Invented. It may describe another institution's policy; we could not tell. |
> | With the document: disclosure is required on every assignment. | Disclosure follows each course's instructions. | Overstated. |
> | With the document: commitment 3, independent competence, is present. | The policy never mentions assessing what students can do without AI. | Invented. |
> | (Not mentioned) | The policy lists approved tools by use. | Omitted. |
>
> Our reading found six of the ten commitments in the text. The AI, given the document, claimed eight. Without
> the document it described a different policy. A student who asked the AI instead of reading would have the
> wrong rule on detectors and on disclosure.

**What separates them**

- "Pretty accurate" is a judgment, not a check. The strong response tests each claim against the text.
- The strong response tested the AI twice, and the two tests found different failures.
- The strong response classifies each discrepancy, and admits where it could not tell the cause.
- It ends with what the errors would cost a student, which is why the exercise matters.

## For instructors

- **Minimum week:** A1 to A3, B1, and C1, then E1 a week later. About ninety minutes of student time.
- **C1 is the anchor.** It uses only public policy text. If your institution has no published policy, use a peer
  institution's and say so.
- **Step 2 is meant to fail sometimes.** Do not penalize students for the AI's errors; grade how they caught them.
- **B2 rates documents, not people.** Keep the discussion on syllabi, not on the instructors who wrote them.
- **Privacy:** B1 reads a tool's terms; no student work is uploaded in this set.
- **Looking ahead:** the [Chapter 19 set](ch19-looking-forward.md) asks what will change, what will stay, and how a
  department would know whether any of this worked.
