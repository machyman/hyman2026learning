# Exercises for Chapter 4: Using AI Well and Badly

*Part I, the student guide. Section numbers refer to v6.1.0 of the book. See [how the exercises are built](../exercise-design.md).*

This chapter is long. The [sample seminar](../README.md) spends three weeks on it, so sections B, C, and D come in
three parts to match: Part 1 (Sections 4.1 and 4.2), Part 2 (4.3 and 4.4), and Part 3 (4.5 and 4.6). The key
points, section A, section E, and the strong-answer example cover the whole chapter.

*Each exercise starts with its assignment category from Section 3.4: NAI (No AI), AIT (AI as Tutor), AIC (AI as Collaborator), or AIS (AI as Object of Study). If your instructor sets a different category, follow theirs.*

## Key points

| | Key point | Section | Exercises |
|---|---|---|---|
| K1 | Good AI use keeps you mentally active and leaves the main work to you. An AI explanation is a bridge, not the authority. Recognition is not recall. | 4.1 | A1, B1, C1, E1 |
| K2 | Put the AI in the critic's seat: you produce, it evaluates. Ask for the challenge by name; a request for neutrality reads as permission to agree. | 4.1.3, 4.1.4 | A2, B1, C1 |
| K3 | In the dialogues, the judgment stays with the student: estimate first, ask for a hint or an audit, and check each critique. An audit can be wrong too. | 4.2 | B1, C1, D1 |
| K4 | Dangerous use often feels helpful at first; the cost comes later. Make first contact yourself, keep the assignment's purpose in view, and do not submit work you cannot explain. | 4.3.1, 4.3.2, 4.3.6 | B2, C5 |
| K5 | Looking right is not being right. A citation can be invented, or real and not support the claim. A revision can change your meaning. Code can run and still be wrong. | 4.2 (Dialogue 6), 4.3.3 to 4.3.5 | A3, C2, C3, D2 |
| K6 | Asking an AI to review its own answer, with nothing new to look at, tends to make it worse. Give it something outside itself. Two systems that agree have told you nothing. | 4.4 | A4, C4 |
| K7 | Verification starts with what you expected. Predict first, then verify in order: same problem, the hard step, sources and shape, then local details. | 4.4 | B3, C3, E2 |
| K8 | Before submitting, ask the six questions (Meaning, Accuracy, Method, Evidence, Assumptions, Ownership). The translation test probes ownership. The checks differ by field. | 4.4, 4.4.1 to 4.4.6 | B3, C3, C5 |
| K9 | Disclosure is a record of process, not a confession. A shared conversation is a record of the interaction, not proof of authorship. | 4.5 | A5, C5, D3, E3 |
| K10 | Before uploading anything, including a thread you share, ask whether you have the right to share it. If unsure, do not upload; ask your instructor. | 4.5.4, 4.6 | A6, B4, C5, E3 |

## A. From memory (about 5 minutes a week)

Answer the two questions for each part in the week you read it, without notes. Then check each answer in the section the key-points table gives for it.

**A1.** *NAI. Part 1.* Section 4.1 calls an AI explanation "a bridge, not the authority." What do you do after reading
one? Why is thinking "that makes sense" weaker than producing the answer?

**A2.** *NAI. Part 1.* Why does Section 4.1.4 ask the AI to challenge you rather than to be neutral? What limits does
the book put on the study behind this advice?

**A3.** *NAI. Part 2.* Name the two citation failures in Section 4.3.3. Which is harder to catch, and why?

**A4.** *NAI. Part 2.* Why does asking an AI to check its own answer tend not to help? Name three things to give it
instead.

**A5.** *NAI. Part 3.* "Disclosure is not a confession." What is it? What two mistakes does it protect you from?

**A6.** *NAI. Part 3.* List five kinds of material Section 4.6 says not to upload without approval. What do you do
when unsure?

## Part 1: Good uses and annotated dialogues (Sections 4.1 and 4.2)

### B. In your own courses

**B1. Who did the producing?** *NAI. About 20 minutes.* List the last three times you used AI for coursework (or three
planned uses). For each, say what you asked, who did the producing, and which good use from Section 4.1 it
matched, if any. Rewrite one prompt so that you produce and the AI evaluates. Name the Section 4.2 dialogue
closest to how you work, and one move from it you will copy.
*Submit:* the three lines, the rewritten prompt, and the move.
*Graded on:* an honest answer to "who did the producing," and whether the rewrite moves that work to you.

### C. With AI

**C1. Teach it to a critic.** *AIT. About 45 minutes.*

1. Choose a concept from a course you are taking. Without notes, write a five-sentence explanation for this
   exercise (not graded work; Section 4.6). Mark the sentence you are least sure of.
2. In a fresh chat with your [Chapter 1](ch01-getting-started.md) configuration, ask for neutral, balanced
   feedback on it.
3. In a second fresh chat, ask for the challenge by name, with a prompt from Section 4.1.3 or 4.1.4.
4. Check every critique against your textbook or notes. Mark each right, wrong, or unclear, as the student does
   in Dialogue 2. Then rewrite your explanation.

In half a page: Did the neutral request read as agreement? Did either reply find your marked sentence? Which
critique was wrong, and how did you know?
*Submit:* both explanations, both links, your marked critiques, and the half page. Add a one-line disclosure.
*Graded on:* the critique-by-critique checks and the rewrite. The AI's replies do not count.

**C2. Watch the certainty.** *AIS. About 25 minutes.* Write a paragraph of four to six sentences
for this exercise, on something from a course you are taking, with at least two hedged claims ("suggests,"
"some," "in this sample"). Predict which words will change. Give it to a fresh AI with the request from Dialogue
6: "Tighten the prose; keep my meaning." List every change in certainty, keep or restore each, and write a
two-sentence author's note like the one in Section 4.4.2.
*Submit:* paragraph, prediction, revision, decisions, and note. Add a one-line disclosure.
*Graded on:* whether you caught every change in certainty, and whether each decision rests on your evidence.

### D. Going further ★

**D1. Annotate your own dialogue.** *AIT. One week.* Annotate a real study session in the style of
Section 4.2: a note at each turn, a one-line takeaway, and a closing disclosure. Mark where the AI was wrong and
how you found out. Include your weak moves; a dialogue with only good moves has been edited, not annotated.
Compare it with one session in [`students/study-sessions/`](../../students/study-sessions/). Redact anything on
the Section 4.6 list. Add a one-line disclosure.

## Part 2: Dangerous uses and verification (Sections 4.3 and 4.4)

### B. In your own courses

**B2. What is this assignment for?** *NAI. About 15 minutes.* For one assignment due soon, answer the two questions in
Section 4.3.6: "What is this assignment trying to teach me? Will this use help me learn that, or help me avoid
it?" Name one AI use that would help you learn it and one that would help you avoid it. Which would tempt you the
night before? Then make first contact: ten minutes on your own attempt, and a note on where you got stuck.
*Submit:* your answers, the two uses, and where you got stuck.
*Graded on:* whether the purpose is specific, and whether the "avoid" use would really tempt you.

**B3. Predict, then keep score.** *NAI. Over one week.* Before you work five course problems, predict each answer, as
Section 4.4 describes: sign, rough size, units, behavior at the extremes. In a course without numbers, predict
what a reading will claim. Then do the work, check it with the tools for your field in Sections 4.4.1 to 4.4.6,
and keep score.
*Submit:* a table of predictions, results, and checks, plus one sentence on what your misses share.
*Graded on:* whether your predictions were specific enough to be wrong.

### C. With AI

**C3. Find what is wrong.** *AIS. About 60 minutes. No AI while you search.*

Two answers in the style of an AI tool. Each has errors planted on purpose, of the kinds Sections 4.3 and 4.4
describe. We do not say how many. Some steps that look suspicious are correct.

1. Read only the problem. Write what a right answer should look like, or work a small case by hand.
2. Read the answer. Verify in the order Section 4.4 gives.
3. For each error: quote the line, say what is wrong and what you checked it against, and give the fix. Rate it
   sure, fairly sure, or a guess.

You may run the code and use your textbook, notes, a library database, and Python's documentation.

**Answer 1: statistics.** Eight students took a 20-point quiz, used a flashcard app for a week, and took a second
20-point quiz on the same topic. Is there evidence that scores changed? Use a two-sided test at the 0.05 level.

| Student | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Before | 11 | 14 | 9 | 15 | 12 | 10 | 13 | 12 |
| After | 13 | 15 | 10 | 17 | 12 | 13 | 14 | 14 |

> We compare the two sets of scores with a two-sample t-test, the test Student (1908) introduced for comparing
> the means of two groups.
>
> 1. *Hypotheses.* H0: the mean score is the same before and after. H1: the means differ.
> 2. *Summary statistics.* Before: mean 12.0, standard deviation 2.00. After: mean 13.5, standard deviation 2.07.
> 3. *Conditions.* With only eight scores in each group, normality matters. Both sets of scores are roughly
>    symmetric with no outliers, so the condition is met.
> 4. *Test statistic.* Pooled standard deviation = √((7 × 2.00² + 7 × 2.07²) / 14) = 2.04. Standard error =
>    2.04 × √(1/8 + 1/8) = 1.02. So t = (13.5 - 12.0) / 1.02 = 1.47, with 14 degrees of freedom.
> 5. *p-value.* Two-sided p = 0.16.
> 6. *Conclusion.* Since 0.16 > 0.05, we fail to reject H0. The flashcard app has no effect on quiz scores.
>
> *Reference:* Student (1908). The probable error of a mean. *Biometrika* 6(1), 1-25.

**Answer 2: Python.** Write a function that approximates the integral of f from a to b with the composite
trapezoid rule, and say how accurate it is.

```python
def trapezoid(f, a, b, n):
    """Approximate the integral of f from a to b using n equal subintervals (n >= 1)."""
    h = (b - a) / n
    total = (f(a) + f(b)) / 2
    for i in range(1, n - 1):
        total += f(a + i * h)
    return total * h
```

> The endpoints get weight 1/2 and the interior points get weight 1, which is the composite trapezoid rule.
>
> *Check:* `trapezoid(lambda x: x, 0, 2, 1)` returns 2.0, the exact value of the integral of x from 0 to 2. The
> function is correct.
>
> *Accuracy:* the trapezoid rule's error is proportional to the step size h, so doubling n halves the error.
>
> *For real work:* use the built-in `math.integrate(f, a, b)`, which applies the same rule and chooses n for you.

*Submit:* your predictions, your findings, and a one-line disclosure (for example, "No AI used; I checked with
Python and my textbook").
*Graded on:* each real error found, explained, and fixed. A correct step marked wrong counts against you unless
you show how you checked it. Part of the grade is calibration: were your "sure" findings right more often than
your guesses?

*Answers:* [ch04-c3-answers.md](ch04-c3-answers.md), for checking yourself after you finish. If your instructor
grades C3, they will give you a fresh pair of answers to search.

**C4. Nothing new, or something outside?** *AIS. About 40 minutes.* Ask a fresh AI to solve a
course problem whose answer you can check. In the same chat, ask it to "review your answer and fix any errors."
In a new chat, give it the problem plus something outside: your textbook's definition, a worked example, or a
program's output. Predict before each step whether the answer will change. In a paragraph: what changed, and
what can two trials tell you about the claim in Section 4.4?
*Submit:* the AI's answers or links, your predictions, and the paragraph. Add a one-line disclosure.
*Graded on:* whether your conclusion stays within what your trials show. The AI's accuracy does not count.

### D. Going further ★

**D2. Five readings, three checks.** *AIS. About two hours. Suited to graduate students.* Ask an
AI for five foundational readings in your field, with what each shows. Name the topic in general terms; paste no
unpublished work or idea of your own (Section 4.6). Check each: Does it exist? Is the record right? Does it say
what the AI said? Sort each failure into one of the two kinds in Section 4.3.3. End as Dialogue 3 does, by asking
your advisor what belongs on the list that is not on it. Add a one-line disclosure.

## Part 3: Disclosure and privacy (Sections 4.5 and 4.6)

### B. In your own courses

**B4. Upload or not?** *NAI. About 20 minutes.* For each case, say whether you would upload it to an AI tool not
approved for that use, which item on the Section 4.6 list applies, and what you would do instead.

1. Your lab partner's half of a joint report, to check that it matches yours.
2. An unpublished dataset from the research group you volunteer in.
3. A practice exam your instructor posted for the class.
4. A screenshot of your study group's chat about the problem set.
5. A proof idea of your own that might become a thesis chapter.
6. A draft recommendation letter a professor asked you to read.

Add one case from your own courses. Then, from its published terms and not from the AI, find out who can see
your conversations in the tool you use most, and how long they are kept (see
[learning tools](../../companion/learning-tools/)).
*Submit:* your seven decisions and the two facts with their sources.
*Graded on:* the right item and a workable alternative for each case. Case 3 has no single right answer; the
grade rests on what you would check first.

### C. With AI

**C5. One session, on the record.** *AIT. About 60 minutes.* This exercise draws on the whole chapter.

1. Choose a short task from a course that allows AI as Tutor. Start a clean thread (Section 4.5.4).
2. Make first contact: write your attempt and predict what a right answer looks like.
3. Use one good use from Section 4.1, named, and ask for the challenge by name.
4. Check one claim the AI makes against something outside the AI.
5. Answer the six questions from Section 4.4, a line each. For Ownership, use the translation test.
6. Without rereading the thread, write a fuller disclosure from the template in Section 4.5.2
   ([`students/disclosure-templates.md`](../../students/disclosure-templates.md)).
7. Reread the thread. Mark where your disclosure was wrong: AI work credited to you, your work credited to the AI,
   or something left out. Correct it.
8. Redact anything on the Section 4.6 list before sharing
   ([`students/sharing-ai-conversations.md`](../../students/sharing-ai-conversations.md)).

*Submit:* attempt and prediction, the claim and your check, the six answers, both disclosures with differences
marked, and the link or redacted transcript. End with a one-line disclosure.
*Graded on:* the differences between your two disclosures, and the claim you checked. The AI's replies do not
count.

### D. Going further ★

**D3. Is the shared thread enough?** *NAI. One page.* Section 4.5.4 calls sharing the conversation "among the
most informative forms of disclosure available to a student," and "a record of the interaction, not proof of
authorship." Should your courses ask for shared threads? Argue from the student's side: what sharing protects,
what it costs in privacy, and what the translation test adds that a link cannot.

## E. One week later (5 minutes)

**E1.** *NAI. After Part 1.* Without notes, explain the concept from C1 in five sentences. Compare with your rewrite.
Which critique stayed with you?

**E2.** *NAI. After Part 2.* Without notes, list the order in which Section 4.4 says to verify. For one C3 error you
found late or missed, say which step would have caught it first.

**E3.** *NAI. After Part 3.* From memory, write the six headings of the fuller disclosure template and five items
from the Section 4.6 list. Check against the chapter.

## What a strong answer looks like: C5

Two condensed responses to C5 from a student in a first-year physics course. The task: a pendulum 1.0 m long is
released from 60 degrees; find its period. Both land near 2 seconds. Only one did the exercise.

**A weak response**

> *Attempt and prediction:* I asked the AI how to find the period of a pendulum.
>
> *Claim checked:* The AI said T = 2π√(L/g) = 2.0 s. I checked the arithmetic on my calculator.
>
> *Six questions:* Yes to all six.
>
> *Disclosure, before and after rereading:* I used AI to understand the problem. I did the work myself.

**A strong response**

> *Attempt and prediction:* Before opening the AI, I used T = 2π√(L/g) and got 2.0 s. I predicted the true period
> would be a little longer, because the formula assumes small angles and 60 degrees is not small.
>
> *Good use:* Find weak points. I asked, "What assumptions am I making that I have not stated?"
>
> *Claim checked:* The AI named the small-angle assumption and said the period is about 7 percent longer, near
> 2.15 s. My textbook derives the formula by replacing sin θ with θ; at 60 degrees, sin θ is 17 percent smaller
> than θ. The textbook's first correction, T ≈ T₀(1 + θ₀²/16), gives 2.14 s, which agrees to within the next
> term.
>
> *Six questions:* Assumptions was the one that mattered. For Ownership, I closed the thread and sketched period
> against release angle: flat near zero, about 7 percent higher at 60 degrees.
>
> *Disclosure from memory:* The AI pointed out the assumption. I found the correction and computed 2.14 s.
>
> *Disclosure after rereading:* Memory was wrong twice. The AI gave 2.15 s before I computed anything, so I
> checked its number rather than producing mine. And the AI first said a correction exists; I looked it up after.
> Corrected: "The AI named the small-angle assumption, said a correction exists, and gave 2.15 s. I found the
> correction in my textbook and used it to check that figure. The prediction and the check are mine."
>
> *Thread:* I had named my lab partner in one turn. I shared a transcript with the name removed and a note.

**What separates them**

- The weak response let the AI make first contact. The strong one made a prediction specific enough to be wrong.
- The weak "check" tested the calculator, not the claim. The strong one asked which assumption was doing the work
  and checked against something outside the AI.
- "Yes to all six" is not an answer. The strong response names the question that mattered.
- The weak disclosure did not change on rereading, so the student did not look. In the strong one, memory had
  credited the student with two things the AI did: a small case of the first mistake Section 4.5.3 names,
  pretending AI was not used when it was. The strong response also caught a classmate's name before sharing.
- Neither is graded on the period. The weak 2.0 s is about 7 percent short, and the student never learned why.

## For instructors

- **Three weeks, one rhythm.** A minimum week: two A questions, one B, one C, and last week's E check. Part 1: A1,
  A2, B1, C1. Part 2: A3, A4, E1, C3. Part 3: A5, A6, E2, C5, then E3. About ninety minutes a week.
- **C3's answers are public.** The two answers were written for this set in the style of AI output, not taken
  from a model, and each planted error is of a kind the chapter names. [The answers](ch04-c3-answers.md) give each
  error, where it is, why it is wrong, and the fix, so students can use C3 for practice and check themselves.
  Students who do not program can do Answer 1 alone.
- **To grade C3, write a fresh pair each term.** Take a problem from your course whose answer you can check.
  Write a solution in the style of AI output, or ask an AI for one and correct it. Plant two to four errors of the
  kinds Sections 4.3 and 4.4 name: a method whose conditions fail, a real source cited for something it does
  not say, a conclusion stronger than the evidence, code that runs and is wrong, a function that does not exist.
  Leave a few correct steps that look suspicious. Check every step yourself, keep your own list, and do not say
  how many errors there are. A fresh pair cannot be looked up, and it keeps the exercise in your course.
- **C5 is the anchor.** Its graded product is the gap between two disclosures, plus a checked claim. An AI can
  draft a disclosure. It cannot tell the student what their memory got wrong.
- **C4 may surprise.** A self-review can fix a wrong answer. Section 4.4 reports a tendency across many trials;
  ask what two trials can show.
- **Graduate sections.** Dialogues 2 and 3 are graduate-level; D2 extends Dialogue 3.
- **Privacy.** No exercise needs anything private uploaded. C1 and C2 use text written for the exercise. C5 uses
  a clean thread and redaction.
- **Looking back and ahead.** C1 reuses the Chapter 1 configuration; B2 returns to the AI-first trap (Section
  1.2). Section 4.1.6 leads into Chapter 5's Oral-Defense Model. The pre-submission checklist is at
  [`students/checklist.md`](../../students/checklist.md).
