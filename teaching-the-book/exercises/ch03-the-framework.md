# Exercises for Chapter 3: The Framework

*Part I, the student guide. Section numbers refer to v6.1.0 of the book. See [how the exercises are built](../exercise-design.md).*

*Each exercise starts with its assignment category from Section 3.4: NAI (No AI), AIT (AI as Tutor), AIC (AI as Collaborator), or AIS (AI as Object of Study). If your instructor sets a different category, follow theirs.*

## Key points

| | Key point | Section | Exercises |
|---|---|---|---|
| K1 | Core Competence, the first CAT dimension, is what you must be able to do without AI. Some tools build a skill you keep; others rent it. How a course uses AI decides which. | 3.1, 3.1.1 | A1, B1, E1 |
| K2 | AI-assisted Practice is a real skill, measured by whether AI helped you think better. Ask for a hint, not a solution; for a critique, not a draft. | 3.1.2 | A1, C2, E2 |
| K3 | Trustworthiness scores what you did, not your character: did you verify, disclose, and own the work? It includes self-calibration, knowing whether you understand the work or it only looks right. | 3.1, 3.1.3 | A1, C1, D2, E1 |
| K4 | The Learning Spiral starts with a question you frame: what you want to know and where you get stuck. Five steps run around it. Each pass should open a deeper question. | 3.2, 3.2.1 | A2, C1 |
| K5 | Try before you ask; a wrong attempt still teaches once feedback arrives. Then ask for help, not replacement. The slide toward replacement shows in what you produce between requests. | 3.2.2, 3.2.3 | C1, C2 |
| K6 | Confidence is not evidence of correctness. Check what matters until you can explain why it is right, rebuild the work with the AI closed, and say what the AI did. | 3.2.4 to 3.2.6 | A3, C1, D2 |
| K7 | When the aim is a new idea, a better output does not mean you became more capable. Write your own ideas first, and keep the choosing for yourself. | 3.3 | D1 |
| K8 | Four categories: No AI, AI as Tutor, AI as Collaborator, and AI as Object of Study. Tutor and Collaborator differ in where the AI's work ends up, not in how well you understand it. | 3.4 | A4, B2, C1 |

## A. From memory (about 10 minutes)

Answer without notes. Then check each answer in the section the key-points table gives for it, and mark what you missed.

**A1.** *NAI.* Name the three dimensions of the CAT framework and say what each asks of you. Which one does the chapter
call the most often misread, and what does it actually score?

**A2.** *NAI.* Sketch the Learning Spiral. Put the right thing at the center, number the five steps, and draw the loop
that runs most often. Why is it a spiral and not a circle?

**A3.** *NAI.* Section 3.2.5 explains why an AI can be wrong and still sound sure. Give the two causes. What follows for
you?

**A4.** *NAI.* Name the four assignment categories and their colors. Why is the fourth off the traffic light? What
moves work from AI as Tutor to AI as Collaborator?

## B. In your own courses

**B1. Build or rent.** *NAI. About 20 minutes.* For one course you are taking, list three things you must be able to
do without AI: the course's Core Competence. Next to each, name the assessment that tests it directly, or write
"none." Then pick one way you use AI in this course now. Does it build a skill you keep, or rent one? What change
would make it build?
*Submit:* the list and one paragraph.
*Graded on:* whether the abilities are specific to the course, and whether your judgment names what you could
still do with the tool gone.

**B2. Sort your assignments.** *NAI. About 15 minutes.* Label three assignments from a course you are taking with the
categories from Section 3.4. Use your instructor's label if there is one; if not, choose one and say why. Then
name one act, on any of the three, that would move your work from AI as Tutor into AI as Collaborator. If any
instructions are unclear, write the question you would ask your instructor.
*Submit:* the labels with reasons, the act, and any question.
*Graded on:* whether your line follows Section 3.4: where the AI's work ends up, not how well you understand it.

## C. With AI

**C1. One pass through the Spiral.** *AIT. About 60 minutes.*

Pick a problem from a course you are taking: homework, a proof, a hard reading, or a bug. The course must allow
AI help on it; if not, use a practice problem on the same topic. Describe it in your own words. Do not upload
course files.

**At the center, frame the question.** Write what you want to know and where your understanding stops. If you
cannot begin, spend five to ten minutes finding the first place you get lost.

1. **Try to solve.** Attempt it before opening any AI, for at least ten to fifteen minutes. For a proof or open
   problem, write the definitions, the goal, one strategy, and where you are stuck. Then write how sure you are,
   from 0 to 100 percent, that your attempt is right.
2. **Ask for help.** Use your configuration from Chapter 1 (B1) and the Study Partner Protocol
   ([`companion/spp/`](../../companion/spp/)). Ask for a hint, a diagnostic question, or a critique, never the
   answer. Before each request, write one line: "What have I produced since the last time I asked?" Take each
   hint back to the problem and try again.
3. **Work independently.** Close the window and finish the work. Explain the key step aloud, or in a second
   form, as in the translation test (Section 2.3).
4. **Verify.** Check the step that matters most without the AI: your textbook, your notes, a second computation,
   running the code, or your instructor.
5. **Reflect and disclose.** Why does each step work? What would change if one assumption were weakened or
   removed? What deeper question did this pass open?

Then, in half a page, compare your confidence from step 1 with what step 4 showed. Say how many times the try,
ask, try again loop ran. Point to the part of your work that shows each CAT dimension.

*Submit:* what you wrote at each step, including the request log, a link to the conversation (checked first for
anything private), and the half page. Add a one-line disclosure.
*Graded on:* the first attempt, the log, and the check. A wrong first attempt costs nothing. The quality of the
AI's replies does not count.

**C2. Rewrite your last question.** *AIT. About 20 minutes.*

Take the last question you asked an AI about coursework, minus anything private. Rewrite it two ways, as Section
3.1.2 suggests: so the AI must give a hint rather than a solution, and so it must critique work you supply. For
the critique, write something short for this exercise, not work you will hand in. Predict what each reply will
ask of you, then run both in a fresh chat.
*Submit:* the original, both rewrites, your predictions, an excerpt of each reply, and three sentences on who did
the thinking. Add a one-line disclosure.
*Graded on:* whether each rewrite keeps the thinking with you.

## D. Going further ★

**D1. A creative pass.** *AIC. One to two hours.* Pick an open task from a course: a design, a
thesis, a project question. Before opening any AI, write three ideas of your own. Then ask an AI for four
alternatives under four different assumptions, with no verdict. Choose one idea yourself and say why you
rejected the rest. Go one step past the task: a new case, a new constraint, or the question it opened. In half
a page, say which list your choice came from and what you could now do without the AI. Add a one-line
disclosure.

**D2. Does confidence track correctness?** *AIS. About an hour.* Write six factual questions
you can check in your course materials: three standard, three about facts that turn up seldom anywhere. Predict
which ones the AI will get right. Ask a fresh AI, note how sure each reply sounds, and check each. In one page,
relate what you found to the two causes in Section 3.2.5. Add a one-line disclosure.

## E. One week later (5 minutes)

**E1.** *NAI.* Write how sure you are that you can still do the key step of your C1 problem. Then do it without notes,
or explain it in five sentences. What stayed, and was your confidence right?

**E2.** *NAI.* From memory, write a hint request and a critique request, like your C2 rewrites, for a problem you are
working on this week.

## What a strong answer looks like: C1

Two condensed responses to C1 from a student in an introductory chemistry course. Both chose the same problem:
how much water forms from 4 grams of hydrogen gas and 16 grams of oxygen gas? Only one did the exercise.

**A weak response**

> *Question:* How much water forms from 4 g of hydrogen and 16 g of oxygen?
>
> *First attempt:* Not sure how to start. Confidence: 50%.
>
> *Request log:* "Where do I start?" "Is hydrogen the one that runs out?" "Show me the first two steps." "What
> is the final answer?" Produced since last ask: notes, each time.
>
> *Check:* I asked the AI whether 18 g was right, and it said yes.
>
> *Reflection:* Each step works because it follows the method. Deeper question: none, the problem is done.
>
> *Half page:* My confidence was about right. The AI was very helpful.

**A strong response**

> *Question:* How do I tell which reactant runs out first? I can balance the equation, but I do not know what to
> compare.
>
> *First attempt:* There is only 4 g of hydrogen against 16 g of oxygen, so hydrogen runs out first. Its 2 mol
> make 2 mol of water, or 36 g. Confidence: 80%.
>
> *Request log:*
> - Produced: the attempt. Asked: "Is my reason for picking hydrogen sound? Do not give the answer." The AI asked
>   whether a gram of each gas holds the same number of molecules.
> - Produced: both amounts in moles, 2 of hydrogen and 0.5 of oxygen. Asked: "What do I compare these
>   against?" It pointed me back to the balanced equation.
>
> *Finished work (window closed):* The equation uses 2 mol of hydrogen per mole of oxygen. So 0.5 mol of oxygen
> needs only 1 mol of hydrogen, and oxygen runs out first. That makes 1 mol of water, 18 g, with 2 g of hydrogen
> left over.
>
> *Check:* Mass in, 4 + 16 = 20 g. Mass out, 18 + 2 = 20 g. My first answer made 36 g of water from 20 g of
> starting material. That is impossible.
>
> *Reflection:* Each step turns the amounts into something the equation can compare. Deeper question: oxygen
> also had fewer moles here. Does the reactant with fewer moles always run out first? I think not. Test case:
> 0.8 mol of hydrogen and 0.5 of oxygen. Hydrogen has more moles but runs out first.
>
> *Half page (excerpt):* I was 80% sure and wrong. What caught it was a question from the AI, not an answer. Core
> Competence shows in the finished work, AI-assisted Practice in the log, and Trustworthiness in the mass check
> and the disclosure.

**What separates them**

- The weak question restates the problem, and the weak attempt commits to nothing, so its 50% can never be
  checked. The strong question says where understanding stops. The strong attempt is wrong at 80%, and that
  gap is the most useful line in the submission.
- The weak log follows the route Section 3.2.3 describes, from a real question to "What is the final answer?"
  The strong log shows something new before every request.
- The weak check asks the AI to grade its own answer. The strong check takes a second road, conservation of
  mass, and shows why the first answer could not be right.
- The weak reflection fits any problem. The strong deeper question comes from this one, and the student built a
  case to test it.

## For instructors

- **Minimum week:** A1 to A4, B2, and C1, then E1 a week later. About ninety minutes of student time.
- **C1 is the anchor.** Its graded product is the first attempt, the record of what the student produced between
  requests, and a check that does not pass through the AI. Say plainly that a wrong first attempt costs nothing,
  or students will polish it with AI before writing it down.
- **Check the category before C1.** If a student uses graded homework, that assignment's own label governs, not
  this exercise's.
- **Why D1 is AI as Collaborator.** The student may choose one of the AI's ideas. Section 3.4 puts that on the
  Collaborator side, even if the student rewords it.
- **Privacy:** no exercise needs anything uploaded. C2's critique uses a piece written for the exercise.
- **Looking back and ahead:** C1 reuses the configuration from Chapter 1's B1 and returns to the illusion of
  competence (Section 2.2.1). Chapter 4 practices the C2 conversion across common uses. To recast your own
  problems so that working them runs the Spiral, see [`companion/spiral-problems/`](../../companion/spiral-problems/).
