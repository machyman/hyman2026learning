# Exercises for Chapter 16: Standards and Requirements

*Part III, the departmental and institutional guide, read from the student's side of the desk. Section numbers
refer to v6.1.1 of the book. See [how the exercises are built](../exercise-design.md). Chapter 16 sets the
standards every course should meet. Read it as a list of what you can expect, and of what you owe.*

*Each exercise starts with its assignment category from Section 3.4: NAI (No AI), AIT (AI as Tutor), AIC (AI as Collaborator), or AIS (AI as Object of Study). If your instructor sets a different category, follow theirs.*

## Key points

| | Key point | Section | Exercises |
|---|---|---|---|
| K1 | Every course must assess some of what you can do on your own. Strong AI-assisted work does not make up for its absence, and this element is not relaxed locally. | 16.1 | D2 |
| K2 | Disclosure should be normal and brief, not a confession. What triggers it is the tool, not the task: a dedicated grammar checker needs no report, but asking a general-purpose AI to check your grammar is AI use. Depth follows the course's core: a tool doing work below what the course teaches can be reported as used and verified. | 16.2 | A1, C1 |
| K3 | Some material must not go into any tool not approved for that use: private information about people, unpublished data, secure assessment materials, confidential documents, private correspondence, restricted course materials, and legally restricted data. Approval is by use, not by tool alone. | 16.3 | A2, B2 |
| K4 | Shared AI conversation threads are student work. They can hold things you told the AI that have nothing to do with the course, so policy should say who sees them, how long they are kept, and what happens when the course ends. | 16.3 | B3 |
| K5 | AI-assisted grading raises four questions an instructor should not settle alone: whether work may leave the institution, what the vendor does with it, what the law requires, and who answers for the grade. A grade that cannot be reconstructed cannot be defended on appeal. | 16.3.1 | B3 |
| K6 | If a course requires AI, you should have an approved tool at no extra cost or an equivalent alternative. Use of AI is uneven across groups, and higher use is not better use. | 16.4 | B2 |
| K7 | Perplexity-based detectors flag non-native writers far more often, and the study that showed it found they were easy to bypass. Detector output should never be the sole or primary evidence. A hallucinated citation is not plagiarism; use the category your code provides. | 16.5 | A3, D1 |
| K8 | The eleven-step procedure rests on process evidence and your own explanation. Without an admission, the evidence often falls short of a formal finding, so educational remedies are usually the route. One alternative design assumes AI use in an open lane and secures a separate lane where capability is observed. | 16.5 | A4, D1, D2 |
| K9 | The framework applies to faculty in full: instructors say where AI shaped what students receive, and do not delegate grades or integrity decisions. Every syllabus should answer five questions. | 16.6, 16.7 | A5, B1 |

## A. From memory (about 10 minutes)

Answer without notes. Then check each answer in the section the key-points table gives for it, and mark what you missed.

**A1.** *NAI.* What triggers a disclosure, the tool or the task? Give one example each way.

**A2.** *NAI.* Name four kinds of material you must not put into an AI tool that is not approved for that use.

**A3.** *NAI.* Why do perplexity-based detectors flag non-native writers more often? What happened when the
vocabulary of those essays was enriched?

**A4.** *NAI.* Describe the two lanes in Section 16.5, and the caution that comes with them.

**A5.** *NAI.* Name the five questions every syllabus should answer.

## B. In your own courses

**B1. Check your syllabi.** *NAI. About 20 minutes.* For two current courses, check the five questions in Section
16.7. Which does each syllabus answer? For each gap, write the question you would ask your instructor.
*Submit:* a two-column table and your questions.
*Graded on:* whether each entry quotes or points to the syllabus.

**B2. Know your approved tools.** *NAI. About 20 minutes.* Find your institution's list of approved AI tools and
the uses each is approved for (Section 16.3). Which may you use with course materials, and which with research
data? If a course requires AI, is a tool provided at no extra cost (Section 16.4)? If there is no list, say where
you looked. Then think back to the last time you used AI for coursework. Did anything you gave it fall under the
barred items?
*Submit:* a short table and two sentences.
*Graded on:* whether the table points to the institution's own pages.

**B3. Where your work goes.** *NAI. About 15 minutes.* For one course, find what it says about two things: shared
AI conversation threads, if the course asks for them, and AI-assisted grading, if it uses it. Using Sections 16.3
and 16.3.1, list what the course tells you and what it leaves out.
*Submit:* two short lists. "The course says nothing" is a valid entry.
*Graded on:* whether each item names the course document it comes from.

## C. With AI

**C1. Disclosure at the right depth.** *NAI, then AIS. About 40 minutes.*

Five uses:

- (a) the spelling checker built into your word processor;
- (b) asking a general-purpose chatbot to fix the grammar of a paragraph;
- (c) an optimizer you ran and checked, in a course where optimization is a means rather than a learning goal;
- (d) a chatbot's suggested fix for a bug in your script, which you kept and tested;
- (e) a chatbot's outline of your argument, in a writing course whose goal is building arguments.

1. **Your decisions (NAI).** For each use, decide whether to disclose and how much: nothing, one line, or a fuller
   account. Cite the rule in Section 16.2 you are applying. For (e), say first what the category would need to be.
2. **The AI's defaults (AIS).** Give the same five uses to an AI and ask what a student should disclose. Do not
   give it the book's rules.
3. **Compare.** Where does the AI's default differ from the standard in Section 16.2? Which is right, and why?

*Submit:* your decisions, the AI's answer (or a shared link), and a paragraph on the differences, with a one-line
disclosure.
*Graded on:* whether each decision cites a rule, and whether the comparison names a real difference.

## D. Going further ★

**D1. A flag is not a finding.** *NAI. One page.* Explain to a classmate why a detector flag is weak evidence,
using the mechanism in Section 16.5 and the caveat that the rates come from 2023 tools. Then list the process
evidence a student should keep as a habit, drawn from steps 3 to 7 of the procedure. Write about mechanisms. Do not
ask anyone about their own language background.

**D2. Your secured lane.** *NAI. One page.* Sort the assessments in two of your courses into a more secure lane and
a less secure lane, as Section 16.5 describes. Does the more secure lane cover what each course says you must be
able to do on your own (Section 16.1)? If not, what is left unobserved?

## E. One week later (about 10 minutes)

**E1.** *NAI.* Without notes, state the rule that triggers disclosure. Name three kinds of process evidence you now
keep, and the five questions every syllabus should answer.

## What a strong answer looks like: C1

Two condensed comparison paragraphs from step 3.

**A weak response**

> The AI and I mostly agreed. It said grammar help does not need to be disclosed because grammar is minor, which
> makes sense. For the outline it said to mention it, so I would add a sentence.

**A strong response**

> We differed on two uses. On (b), the AI said grammar help needs no disclosure because it is a small task. Section
> 16.2 says the tool triggers disclosure, not the task: a dedicated checker needs no report, but a general-purpose
> AI asked to fix grammar does. One line covers it. My own reason for the rule: a chatbot asked for grammar can
> change the wording too, and nobody can tell what it changed unless I say so.
>
> On (e), the AI suggested one sentence. But the outline did thinking the course exists to develop, so it needs a
> fuller account (Section 16.2). Before that, the category has to allow it at all. Under NAI or AIT, an
> AI-drafted outline is not permitted, and no disclosure makes it so.
>
> We agreed on (a), (c), and (d). For (c), a line saying the optimizer was used and checked is enough, because the
> course does not teach how optimizers work.

**What separates them**

- The weak response accepts the AI's reason without checking it against the standard.
- The strong response finds the case where the AI judged by task, and names the rule that judges by tool.
- The strong response separates two questions for (e): is the use permitted, and how much must be said.
- The strong response labels its own reasoning as its own, apart from what the chapter says.

## For instructors

- **Minimum week:** A1 to A5, B1, and C1, then E1 a week later. About two hours of student time.
- **C1 is the anchor.** Collect step 1 before students open a tool. The cases are taken from Section 16.2, so the
  standard is not in dispute; the AI's defaults are.
- **D1 is sensitive.** Keep it about how detectors work. Do not run student work through a detector as part of
  this set, and do not ask students about their language background.
- **B2 and B3 may find gaps.** A missing list or a silent syllabus is a finding, and a useful one for you.
- **Privacy:** C1 uses invented cases, not student work.
- **Looking ahead:** the [Chapter 17 set](ch17-implementation-and-change.md) reads training, governance, and the
  rollout from the student's side, including the AI-literacy outcome every student should meet.
