# Exercises for Chapter 7: The Student's Toolkit

*Part I, the student guide. Section numbers refer to v6.1.0 of the book. See [how the exercises are built](../exercise-design.md). Chapter 7 closes Part I, so this set puts its templates to work on real coursework.*

*Each exercise starts with its assignment category from Section 3.4: NAI (No AI), AIT (AI as Tutor), AIC (AI as Collaborator), or AIS (AI as Object of Study). If your instructor sets a different category, follow theirs.*

## Key points

| | Key point | Section | Exercises |
|---|---|---|---|
| K1 | Attempt before you open the tool, and ask for a hint, not the answer. Close the AI window before you write the final version. If you cannot explain a step in your own words, do not submit yet. | 7.1 | A1, B2, C1, D3 |
| K2 | Write the step you keep skipping as a when-then plan: a trigger and an action. | 7.1 | A1, B2, E2 |
| K3 | In a group, each member attempts alone first, and the AI gets the disagreement, not a blank page. The independent attempts decide whether the group builds understanding or hides its lack. | 7.1 | D2 |
| K4 | Configuration changes how the AI responds, not what it knows. A confident, correct answer can still narrow your question, so ask the AI to name the conditions an answer depends on. Keep one configuration across your degree and grow it. | 7.2 | A2, B1, C2 |
| K5 | Execution is now cheap. Specification and verification are the skills that survive a change of tool. The AI use log records what you rejected and what you can now explain, not only what you used. | 7.4, 7.5 | A3, C1 |
| K6 | In research, disclosure becomes a professional rule, verification has no solution to compare against, and authorship is earned by judgment. | 7.7 | A4, D1 |
| K7 | When research stalls, do not ask the tool what to do next. Name the bottleneck, list at most three moves, ask what each would teach you if it failed, and write a sentence saying why you chose one. | 7.7 | C3, D1, E3 |
| K8 | The blank-page item is the strongest single calibration check on the checklist. Rebuilding work from a blank page is harder to fake than explaining it while you look at it. | 7.10 | C1, E1 |

## A. From memory (about 10 minutes)

Answer without notes. Then check each answer in the section the key-points table gives for it, and mark what you missed.

**A1.** *NAI.* List the seven steps of the routine in Section 7.1, in order. Which one do you skip most often? Write it
as a when-then plan.

**A2.** *NAI.* Ask an AI for the limit of sin x / x as x approaches zero and you get 1, which is correct. What does the
answer leave out? What instruction does Section 7.2 add to the configuration because of it?

**A3.** *NAI.* Section 7.4 says execution is now cheap. Which two skills stay expensive? Name two fields of the AI use
log in Section 7.5 that record something other than what you used.

**A4.** *NAI.* Name the three things that change when the work is research. In coursework you can check against a
solution. Name two checks that remain when no solution exists.

## B. In your own courses

**B1. A living configuration.** *NAI. About 20 minutes.* Compare the configuration you wrote for Chapter 1 with the
six categories in Section 7.2. Add the last instruction of category 3, and decide whether you need category 6.
Then add one instruction for a failure you have actually hit, and cut one line that no longer applies.
*Submit:* the revised configuration, with each change marked and a sentence on what prompted it.
*Graded on:* whether each change traces to something that happened to you, not to the template.

**B2. The step you skip.** *NAI. About 15 minutes.* For the last assignment you finished in a course you are taking,
mark each step of the routine in Section 7.1 as done, partly done, or skipped. Pick the skipped step that cost
you most, and name the pitfall from Section 7.6 it invites. Write that step as a when-then plan, with a trigger
you will meet this week.
*Submit:* your marks, the pitfall, and the plan.
*Graded on:* whether the trigger is a moment you will notice. "When I study, then I try harder" fails.

## C. With AI

**C1. One assignment, start to finish.** *AIT. One real assignment this week, plus about 45 minutes.*

1. Choose an assignment in a course you are taking whose rules allow at least AI as Tutor. If the rule is
   unclear, ask first, with a specific question like those in Section 6.2.
2. Predict. Which step of the routine will you be most tempted to skip? From 1 to 5, how sure are you that you
   can work a nearby problem without AI at step 6?
3. Run steps 1 to 6 of the routine with your configuration from B1, your plan from B2, and at least one prompt
   from Section 7.3. At step 2, upload nothing: type the key points of your own notes and have the AI quiz you
   on them.
4. Keep an AI use log (Section 7.5), one entry per session. Fill in "Output rejected or changed" every time.
5. At step 5, check one claim, step, or number from the AI against your textbook, notes, or own calculation.
6. Before you submit, run the checklist in Section 7.10 ([printable copy](../../students/checklist.md)). For its
   blank-page item, redo the key step with AI and notes closed.

Then answer, in half a page:

- How did your prediction compare with what happened?
- Which "rejected or changed" entry taught you the most?
- What did the blank-page redo show that rereading your finished work did not?

*Submit:* your prediction, the log, the claim you checked and how, your nearby problem, the redo, and your half
page, but not the assignment itself. Add a one-line disclosure.
*Graded on:* the log's record of what you rejected and how you checked, and how honestly you compare your
prediction and your redo with what happened. The assignment's own grade does not count.

**C2. Confident, right, and narrower.** *AIS. About 30 minutes.* From a course you are taking,
write two questions whose full answer depends on something the question leaves out, such as a condition, a
definition, or the fact that a number is only approximate. Write what a complete answer must mention. Ask both
questions with your configuration turned off, then with your configuration from B1. For each reply, record
whether it was correct, judged by your textbook or your own work, and whether it named what was left out.
*Submit:* your questions, the four replies (shared links or short excerpts), your record, and three sentences
on what the configuration changed and what it did not. Add a one-line disclosure.
*Graded on:* whether your questions really leave something out, and whether your check stands without the AI.

**C3. One Next-Move Check.** *AIC. About 40 minutes. For a course project, thesis, or research
project.* Check first that your course or advisor allows AI for this work.

1. Without AI, write your current question and name the bottleneck: a missing fact, a doubtful assumption, an
   untested computation, or too many options.
2. Without AI, list at most three next moves. For each, write what it would teach you if it failed.
3. Give the AI your diagnosis and your moves, in general terms, with no unpublished data, code, or results
   (Section 4.6). Ask it for a move you missed and for its case against your diagnosis. Do not ask it what to
   do next. The prompt in the [Next-Move Memo](../../instructors/next-move-memo.md) works well.
4. Choose one move, write one sentence saying why, and do it.

*Submit:* the memo, on the Next-Move Memo template, with the AI's strongest objection and your reply. Leave the
Result row for E3. Add a one-line disclosure.
*Graded on:* whether the bottleneck is a diagnosis, not a restatement of the task; whether the moves differ in
what failure would teach; and whether your sentence says what this move shows that the others would not.

## D. Going further ★

**D1. A research record.** *AIC. Three to four weeks of a thesis or research project.* Ask your
advisor what they want disclosed, and in what form. Keep a record as you go: which tool, on which part, and what
you did with the output. Run C3 whenever the work stalls. Check one result in two of the ways Section 7.7
names, neither resting on an AI. Submit the record, the disclosure paragraph a journal would ask for, and a page
on what the checks found. End with a one-line disclosure.

**D2. Bring the AI the disagreement.** *AIT. A group of two to four, about one hour.* Choose a practice
problem from a shared course whose rules allow AI as Tutor. Each member attempts it alone for fifteen minutes.
Write the disagreement between your attempts as one question the group agrees on, and bring the AI that
question, not anyone's attempt. A member who did not write the prompt checks the reply against the course text.
Then each member answers alone: did the AI settle the disagreement or just pick a side, and can you explain the
parts you did not work on? Submit the question, the check, the answers, and one joint one-line disclosure.

**D3. Your toolkit on one page.** *NAI. One page.* The chapter is written for "the student with a question at
eleven at night." Write the page you would want then: your key configuration lines, your when-then plan, the
prompts you actually use, and the line of the compact in Section 7.9 you find hardest to keep. Beside each line,
name the chapter, 1 to 6, that gives its reason.

## E. One week later (5 to 10 minutes)

**E1.** *NAI.* Without notes, redo the key step from C1 from a blank page. Compare it with last week's redo. What
stayed, and what went?

**E2.** *NAI.* Write your when-then plan from memory. How often did the trigger come this week, and how often did you
act on it? If it never fired, rewrite the trigger.

**E3.** *NAI.* If you did C3, state your bottleneck and chosen move from memory. Then fill in the memo's Result and
Revised question rows.

## What a strong answer looks like: C1

Two condensed responses to C1 from students in a differential equations course. Both ran the routine on the
same problem set. Only one did the exercise.

**A weak response**

> *Prediction:* I will skip the before-class step. 4 out of 5 on the nearby problem.
>
> *Log:* Prompt: "Help me with problem 4." Useful output: it walked me through the solution. Output rejected or
> changed: nothing. Verification method: matched the back of the book. What I can explain without AI: all of it.
>
> *Nearby problem and redo:* I did problem 5 without AI. I redid problem 4 and got the same answer.
>
> *Half page:* My prediction was right. The routine is useful but slow. The blank page showed I understood the
> problem.

**A strong response**

> *Prediction:* I will be tempted to skip step 5, because answers in this course check by substitution. Nearby
> problem without AI: 2 out of 5.
>
> *Log, session 2:* Problem 4: solve y' = y², y(0) = 1. I stalled on the constant. Prompt: "Give me one hint
> about the main obstacle. Do not solve the problem." Useful output: apply the initial condition first.
> Rejected: the AI's offer to finish the problem. I finished it myself.
>
> *Claim checked:* At step 5 the AI said my answer, y = 1/(1 - x), had no gaps. Substitution confirms the
> formula. But it blows up at x = 1, and our textbook's section on existence says the solution lives only on the
> interval containing the starting point. So the answer holds for x < 1. The AI was right about the formula and
> silent about the interval.
>
> *Nearby problem:* Problem 6, y' = 2y², y(0) = 1, with AI closed. The formula took 9 minutes. The interval,
> x < 1/2, took 4 more.
>
> *Blank-page redo:* I wrote "for x ≠ 1," which is wrong: the solution cannot cross the blow-up. My submitted
> answer was right only because I had just read the textbook.
>
> *Half page:* I was right about step 5 and too pessimistic about step 6. The rejected offer taught me the most,
> because it came just when I felt I had it. The redo showed what rereading hid: I still confuse where a formula
> is defined with where a solution exists. New configuration line: "For differential equations, state the
> interval on which a solution exists." New plan: when an answer checks by substitution, then I ask where it
> stops being valid.
>
> *Disclosure:* I used the course's AI tool for a hint and a gap check on problem 4, and verified the answer
> against the textbook.

**What separates them**

- The weak log records only what was used. "Rejected or changed: nothing" beside a walked-through solution
  means the student kept all of it. The strong log records the offer it turned down, which is what the log is
  for (Section 7.5).
- Matching the back of the book confirms the AI's answer, not the student's understanding. The strong check asks
  a question substitution cannot answer, names its source, and finds what the AI left out.
- The weak prediction and redo report no detail, so there is no gap to learn from. The strong ones report
  numbers, admit where the prediction missed, and find an error the submitted work hid. Rebuilding is harder
  to fake than rereading.
- The strong response feeds the lesson back into the configuration and the when-then plan. The weak one ends
  with the assignment.

## For instructors

- **Minimum week:** A1 to A4, B2, and C1, then E1 and E2 a week later. About ninety minutes of student time,
  beyond the assignment C1 runs inside.
- **C1 is the anchor and the capstone of Part I.** It puts configuration, calibration, the Learning Spiral,
  verification, and disclosure (Chapters 1 to 4) to work on real coursework. An AI can write the half page. It
  cannot produce the gap between the submitted work and the blank-page redo.
- **The other course's rule governs C1** (Section 7.2). If all of a student's graded work this week is No AI,
  use practice problems from a course text. Collect C1 after the other course's deadline.
- **Graduate students:** Section 7.7 is the reason to assign C3 and D1. Pair them with the [Next-Move
  Memo](../../instructors/next-move-memo.md), whose grading questions match C3's. Undergraduates new to research
  can start with [undergraduate research with AI](../../students/undergraduate-research.md). In the ten-week
  seminar in [Teaching the book](../README.md), this set is week 9.
- **Privacy:** Section 7.1 has students bring posted or photographed notes into a course project. C1 has them
  type their own notes instead, so no course material is uploaded without permission (Section 4.6). C3 and D1
  keep unpublished research out of the AI, and D2 gives it the group's question, not a classmate's attempt.
- **Worth a minute in class:** Section 7.8 says that if AI helped grade a student's work, the student should be
  told what it did, know that a person decided the grade, and be able to ask that person to look again. Ask
  whether students' courses meet that standard.
- **Looking back:** B1 revises the configuration from the [Chapter 1 set](ch01-getting-started.md), and C1 and
  E1 repeat the blank-page rehearsal from Section 2.2.
