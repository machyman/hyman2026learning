# Exercises for Chapter 6: Judgment and Limits

*Part I, the student guide. Section numbers refer to v6.1.1 of the book. See [how the exercises are built](../exercise-design.md).*

*Each exercise starts with its assignment category from Section 3.4: NAI (No AI), AIT (AI as Tutor), AIC (AI as Collaborator), or AIS (AI as Object of Study). If your instructor sets a different category, follow theirs.*

## Key points

| | Key point | Section | Exercises |
|---|---|---|---|
| K1 | Sometimes the best use of AI is not to use it. Of the five cases the chapter names, the common one is when AI would make you skip the part of the work you most need to learn. | 6.1 | A1, B1, C1, E1 |
| K2 | Watch your own pattern. Use with a purpose is you using the tool. Use out of habit is the tool using you. "Use AI when it helps you think. Stop when it starts thinking for you." | 6.1, 6.6 | A2, B2, E1 |
| K3 | Some learning comes from people. Use AI to prepare for those conversations, not to avoid them. Find someone who will argue with you. | 6.1 | D1 |
| K4 | A student may choose not to use AI at all. The framework still applies: every assignment is No AI, and the book's terms name what that student will face. | 6.1.1 | D2 |
| K5 | When unsure, ask before AI touches work you will submit. Ask about the role you want AI to play, not "Can I use AI?" If you already used it, tell your instructor early. | 6.2 | B3 |
| K6 | In group work, agree on the category and the rules before you start, keep your own verification notes, and disclose together. Do not hand off the part you most need to learn. | 6.3 | A4, C2 |
| K7 | The design of AI use decides whether learning improves, stays flat, or declines, and students often do not notice the loss. The practical test: after using AI, can you explain more, verify more, and do more on your own? | 6.4 | A3, C1, E1 |
| K8 | AI detection tools are not perfect. Your best protection is process evidence: honest disclosure, draft history, verification notes, and the ability to explain your work. You can expect four visible commitments from your institution. | 6.1, 6.5 | D2, E2 |

## A. From memory (about 10 minutes)

Answer without notes. Then check each answer in the section the key-points table gives for it, and mark what you missed.

**A1.** *NAI.* Section 6.1 names five times not to use AI. List as many as you can. Which one does the chapter call
common? Give an example of it from your own courses.

**A2.** *NAI.* Name and describe the two kinds of AI use the chapter asks you to sort a day into. What should you ask
yourself before you reach for AI?

**A3.** *NAI.* In the trial by Bastani and colleagues, what happened to practice and exam performance with an
unrestricted AI interface? With a guardrailed tutor? What does the chapter say not to conclude from the numbers?

**A4.** *NAI.* In Section 6.3, what does "collaboration" mean, and how does it differ from the AI as Collaborator
category? What three things does a group's joint disclosure state?

## B. In your own courses

**B1. The part you most need.** *NAI. About 15 minutes.* For one course you are taking, name the struggle the course
is trying to teach you to handle, the part you would most like to skip. Say in two sentences how AI could let you
skip it. Then write a rule for yourself in the form of the chapter's examples: "If you are weak at algebra, do
not use AI to bypass algebra practice." Add it to your configuration from the [Chapter 1 set](ch01-getting-started.md).
*Submit:* the part, how AI could skip it, and your rule.
*Graded on:* whether the rule names a specific task and is one you could follow this week.

**B2. Sort a day.** *NAI. About 15 minutes.* For one day of coursework this week, list each time you used AI or
reached for it. Mark each *purpose* (a specific question, a draft to test, a step you were stuck on) or *habit*
(you took what it gave and moved on). For each habit entry, answer the chapter's questions: what were you trying
to do, and could you have done it yourself? Leave out anything personal.
*Submit:* the sorted list with your answers.
*Graded on:* whether each entry is specific enough to sort. Many habit entries cost no points.

**B3. Ask the right question.** *NAI. About 10 minutes.* Pick an assignment this term where the AI rule is unclear to
you. Write the question you would send your instructor. Like the examples in Section 6.2, it should name the role
you want AI to play and how you would show your use. If the rule really is unclear, send it.
*Submit:* your question, and the reply if you sent it.
*Graded on:* whether the question names a specific role for AI and a way to show the use.

## C. With AI

**C1. Two kinds of practice, one test.** *AIT, one session only. About 60 minutes, in two sittings a few
days apart.*

1. In a course you are taking, choose two kinds of task from one unit you have not mastered: two problem types,
   two kinds of source, or two kinds of bug. For each kind, pick two tasks of similar difficulty. Practice on
   one. Hold the other back, unseen, for the test. Use practice work your course allows AI on.
2. Practice the first kind with no AI, using your textbook and notes. Record where you got stuck and how you got
   unstuck.
3. Practice the second kind with AI as Tutor, using your configuration from Section 1.2. Ask for help, not
   answers. Save a shared link to the conversation
   ([`students/sharing-ai-conversations.md`](../../students/sharing-ai-conversations.md)). Record where you got
   stuck and what the AI gave you.
4. Predict your score on each held-back task, from 1 to 5, with one sentence of reasoning for each.
5. Two or three days later, do both held-back tasks with no AI and no notes. Check them against your textbook,
   an answer key, or your instructor.

Then answer, in half a page:

- Did your predictions match your results? If not, what misled you?
- When did the AI hand you something you would otherwise have had to produce? Did it show on the test?
- Apply the practical test from Section 6.4 to the AI session.
- What besides AI could explain the difference?

*Submit:* your session notes, the conversation link, your predictions, your two test attempts with how you
checked them, and your half page. Add a one-line disclosure.
*Graded on:* how precisely you compare prediction with result, and the moment you name. Test scores do not count.

**C2. One project, one disclosure.** *AIC. Groups of three, on a short task your instructor sets.
About 90 minutes, plus a three-minute presentation.*

1. Before you start, agree in writing how AI may be used and on which parts. Your group may set tighter rules
   than the category, not looser ones.
2. Each member names, in one line, the part of the task they most need to learn, and takes on some of it. Try
   the hard part before asking AI.
3. Each member keeps verification notes for their own part. Do not paste a teammate's work into an AI.
4. Write one joint disclosure: what the team used AI for, what each member verified, and what the group changed
   or set aside ([`students/disclosure-templates.md`](../../students/disclosure-templates.md)).
5. Present. Lead with a moment the tool handed you something plausible that you did not accept, and put the
   output beside what you changed. A group that checked nothing has nothing to show (Section 6.3).
6. Swap check. Without notes or AI, each member explains a part a teammate did. Your instructor picks the part.

*Submit:* the agreement, the verification notes, the presentation outline, and the joint disclosure, which
stands in for individual disclosure lines.
*Graded on:* whether the disclosure matches the notes, the rejected output you show, and each member's swap.

## D. Going further ★

**D1. Someone who argues.** *NAI. Two weeks.* Twice in two weeks, explain a method or argument from your course
to a classmate who agrees to ask why it works, not just whether the answer matches. Then switch roles. Note the
weakest step each of you found. In one page, test the chapter's claim that the student who learns to find the
weak step in a friend's argument is the one who catches the confident mistake in a chatbot's. Also note any time
you used AI to avoid a conversation rather than to prepare for one.

**D2. From where you sit.** *NAI. One page.* For your own university, find evidence that each commitment in
Section 6.5 is real, or record that it is missing. Then read the list as a student who has chosen not to use AI
at all (Section 6.1.1). Which commitment matters most to that student, and which situation from Section 6.1.1
does it answer? End with one request you would make, to whom, and how anyone would know it was met.

## E. One week later (about 10 minutes)

**E1.** *NAI.* Without notes, list the five times not to use AI and state the practical test from Section 6.4. Then sort
yesterday's AI use as you did in B2. Has the mix changed?

**E2.** *NAI.* Without notes, list the four kinds of process evidence from Section 6.1. For the last assignment you
submitted, which could you show today?

## What a strong answer looks like: C1

Two condensed responses to C1 from students in a calculus course. Both practiced integration by parts without AI
and partial fractions with AI as Tutor. Only one did the exercise.

**A weak response**

> *Predictions:* Integration by parts: 3. Partial fractions: 4.
>
> *Sessions:* Integration by parts took a while. Partial fractions with the AI was faster and clearer.
>
> *Test:* Both mostly right.
>
> *Half page:* The AI session was more efficient, and the explanation was clear, so I understood partial
> fractions better. My predictions were about right. AI is a useful tool when used responsibly.
>
> *Disclosure:* I used AI for this assignment.

**A strong response**

> *Predictions:* Integration by parts: 3. I got stuck choosing *u* and needed the textbook's worked example.
> Partial fractions: 5. Every step the AI showed made sense as I read it.
>
> *Sessions:* Integration by parts took 25 minutes. My first *u* made the integral worse, so I switched. Partial
> fractions took 12 minutes. I asked for a hint on the setup. The hint wrote out the full decomposition, with
> the coefficients solved on the next line. I read it, agreed, and moved on.
>
> *Test, three days later:* Integration by parts correct. Partial fractions wrong: the denominator had a repeated
> factor, and I wrote one term for it instead of two. The textbook's worked solution showed the missing term.
>
> *Half page:* My predictions were backward. Partial fractions felt smooth because the hint did the setup, the
> one step I most needed to practice. On the test I could copy a form I had seen but not build a new one. By the
> chapter's test, the AI session did not let me explain or do more on my own. It let me recognize more. One
> caution: the repeated factor made the test problem harder, so part of the gap may be difficulty. Next time I
> will tell the AI to ask me what form the denominator needs, not show it.
>
> *Disclosure:* I used an AI tutor on the partial-fractions practice problem only (link above); everything else
> was done without AI.

**What separates them**

- The weak predictions give no reasons. The strong ones do, and one of those reasons turns out to be the error.
- The weak notes record speed. The strong notes name the moment the tool took over the step the student needed
  to practice.
- The weak half page rests on "mostly right" and a feeling of clarity. The strong one sets prediction beside
  result, applies the test from Section 6.4, and names a rival explanation.
- The weak disclosure says nothing about what the AI did. The strong one says where AI was used and where not.

## For instructors

- **Minimum week:** A1 to A4, B1, and C1, then E1 a week later. About ninety minutes of student time.
- **C1 is the anchor.** Its graded product is a prediction set beside a later result, plus one named moment. The
  delayed test is the only part that measures learning. Run it in class if you can, so it is done without AI.
- **Expect varied results in C1.** Some students will do better on the AI-practiced task. That is not a failed
  exercise: Section 6.4 says design decides, not that AI always hurts. Ask what kept the student working.
- **Students who do not use AI** (Section 6.1.1) can do C1 by practicing the second task with a classmate or at
  office hours, and C2 under No AI. In B2 they can sort any help they sought.
- **C2** needs a task a group can finish in one sitting. It can run under another category if you set it before
  the group starts (Section 6.3). Choose the swap part on the spot.
- **Privacy and accommodations:** no exercise needs anything uploaded, and C2 forbids pasting a teammate's work
  into an AI. No exercise should make a student reveal an accommodation. A student whose approved assistive tool
  depends on AI may need the no-AI parts of C1 adjusted (Section 6.2).
- **Looking ahead:** Section 7.8 returns to what to do when unsure, and the AI use log in Section 7.5 is one way
  to keep the process evidence E2 asks about. C1 reuses the Chapter 1 configuration. In C2, the translation test
  from Section 2.3 still applies to each member's part, as Section 6.3 notes.
