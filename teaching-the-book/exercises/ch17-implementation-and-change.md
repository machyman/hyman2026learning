# Exercises for Chapter 17: Implementation and Change

*Part III, the departmental and institutional guide, read from the student's side of the desk. Section numbers
refer to v6.1.1 of the book. See [how the exercises are built](../exercise-design.md). Chapter 17 is about how
an institution gets from a written policy to changed practice. Students appear in it more often than you might
expect: on the body that owns the policy, in the pilots, in the annual review, and as the people the support
units serve.*

*Each exercise starts with its assignment category from Section 3.4: NAI (No AI), AIT (AI as Tutor), AIC (AI as Collaborator), or AIS (AI as Object of Study). If your instructor sets a different category, follow theirs.*

## Key points

| | Key point | Section | Exercises |
|---|---|---|---|
| K1 | The questions AI policy raises are academic and ethical, not technical, so a policy left to IT leaves them unowned. A standing body led from the academic side, with students among its members, should own the policy, and the policy should carry a date and a review cadence. | 17.1.1 | A1, B3 |
| K2 | Much of learning to use AI well happens outside any course: in the library, the writing center, tutoring, advising, and disability services. | 17.1.2 | B1 |
| K3 | A department should not make every instructor use the same AI policy. It should make every course state its policy clearly. | 17.2 | B3 |
| K4 | Orientation should teach the Learning Spiral, what not to upload, how to check sources, and how to prepare for no-AI assessments. | 17.4.1 | A2, B2 |
| K5 | The case for a required AI-literacy outcome rests on equity: students who use AI least are the least likely to take an optional offering. Students ask for it too, ahead of every punitive option in one 2025 survey. It can be delivered through orientation and the four course categories rather than a new course. | 17.4.2 | A3, C1 |
| K6 | Pilots and the annual review collect feedback from students. Instructors should revisit their course policy each time they teach. | 17.5 | B3 |
| K7 | AI-aware teaching costs money and faculty time. The cheapest path shifts the cost from the budget to the workload, and where a course requires AI, tool licenses become a cash cost. | 17.5.5 | C1 |
| K8 | Faculty resistance is mostly practical, not ideological. Spot checks of process records keep the workload down, and students should be told they are in place. Three patterns consistently disappoint: training without local input, detector-only enforcement, and requirements blind to discipline. | 17.6 | A4, D1 |
| K9 | Institutional policy often grows from departments that built coherent practice first. A disciplinary society can supply the text; the department supplies the authority. | 17.7 | D2 |

## A. From memory (about 10 minutes)

Answer without notes. Then check each answer in the section the key-points table gives for it, and mark what you missed.

**A1.** *NAI.* Why should AI policy not be left to information technology? Who should sit on the body that owns
it?

**A2.** *NAI.* Name the six moves of the Learning Spiral as Section 17.4.1 lists them for orientation.

**A3.** *NAI.* Give the equity case for making AI literacy a required outcome rather than an optional one.

**A4.** *NAI.* Name the three patterns that consistently disappoint. Give an example of a requirement that suits
one discipline and not another.

## B. In your own courses

**B1. Map your support.** *NAI. About 30 minutes.* For each of the five units in Section 17.1.2, find what your
institution offers on AI: a workshop, a guide, a consultation, or nothing you could find. Which would you use
first, and for what?
*Submit:* a five-row table with links, and two sentences.
*Graded on:* whether each row links to the unit's own page.

**B2. Your orientation.** *NAI. About 15 minutes.* Did your orientation or onboarding teach anything about AI?
Check what it covered against Section 17.4.1. Then write three sentences on what you would add for the students who
arrive next year.
*Submit:* a short checklist and three sentences. "Nothing about AI" is a valid finding.
*Graded on:* whether the additions are specific enough to put in a module.

**B3. Where students have a voice.** *NAI. About 20 minutes.* Find the body at your institution that owns AI policy,
if there is one (Section 17.1.1). Does it include students? Is the policy dated, with a review cycle? Then find the
route by which a student can raise an AI-policy question: a student government committee, a senate seat, a
department's student advisory group, or a course evaluation question.
*Submit:* the bodies and the route, named by office, not by person.
*Graded on:* whether a classmate could follow the route you describe.

## C. With AI

**C1. A student request.** *NAI, then AIT for the critique. About 60 minutes, in groups of three.*

1. Choose one thing students could reasonably ask their institution for, using Sections 17.4.2 and 17.7.5: a
   required AI-literacy outcome; an approved tool at no cost where a course requires AI; notice of spot checks;
   or student seats on the body that owns AI policy.
2. Without AI, draft a one-page request, written as students, to the body or route you found in B3. Say what you
   ask for, why, what it would cost, and how anyone would know it worked. Tie every factual claim to the book or
   to your institution's own documents.
3. Ask an AI to act as a skeptical member of the receiving committee and list the three strongest objections. Ask
   it not to rewrite your request.
4. Revise the request yourselves, and answer the strongest objection in it.

*Submit:* the draft, the AI's objections (or a shared link), the revised request, and a one-line disclosure.
Whether you send it is your choice and not part of the grade.
*Graded on:* whether each claim traces to evidence, whether the cost is stated honestly (Section 17.5.5), and
whether the strongest objection is answered rather than ignored.

## D. Going further ★

**D1. The view from the front of the room.** *NAI. One page.* Choose two barriers from Section 17.6.3. For each,
describe what a student would notice in a course where it has not been addressed. Then name one thing students can
do that lowers its cost for the instructor, such as a clean disclosure block or a process record kept as you go.
Write about patterns, not about your instructors.

**D2. Who holds the pen in your field?** *NAI. One page.* Section 17.7.3 contrasts the department and the
disciplinary society. Find whether a society in your field has published guidance on AI in teaching. Does it supply
text a department could adopt, such as model assignments or a statement of what independent competence means? What
would your department still have to decide for itself?

## E. One week later (about 10 minutes)

**E1.** *NAI.* Without notes, name the six moves of the Learning Spiral and two support units you would use, and
for what. Then name the route by which a student at your institution can raise an AI-policy question.

## What a strong answer looks like: C1

Two condensed requests for a required AI-literacy outcome.

**A weak response**

> Students need AI training. Everyone uses AI and it is the future. The university should create a mandatory AI
> course for all students. This will help students succeed.

**A strong response**

> *What we ask:* that AI literacy become a required learning outcome, met through orientation and the four
> assignment categories in our courses, not through a new course (Section 17.4.2).
>
> *Why required:* students do not arrive equally familiar with these tools. Survey evidence cited in Section
> 17.4.2 finds low-income, underrepresented, and female students less likely to use them regularly, so an optional
> workshop reaches those who need it least. Students want this: in a 2025 survey of more than a thousand students,
> education on ethical AI use ranked among the most-wanted responses, ahead of every punitive option.
>
> *Cost:* a short module in the onboarding slot our institution already uses for required modules. The larger cost
> is faculty time to label assignments, and we do not pretend it is free (Section 17.5.5).
>
> *How you would know:* one question in next year's orientation survey, and the share of syllabi that label their
> assignments.
>
> *The objection we expect:* "another required module that students click through." Our answer: the module is
> the floor. The depth comes from the course categories, which is why we ask for an outcome, not a course.

**What separates them**

- "It is the future" is not a reason a committee can act on. The strong request gives two: equity and demand,
  each traced to evidence.
- The weak request asks for the most expensive form. The strong one asks for the form the chapter recommends and
  says why.
- The strong request admits a cost it cannot make disappear.
- It names a measure of success, and answers the objection a committee would actually raise.

## For instructors

- **Minimum week:** A1 to A4, B1, B3, and C1, then E1 a week later. About two hours of student time.
- **C1 is the anchor.** Grade the request on evidence, honesty about cost, and the answer to the objection. Do not
  grade the position taken, and do not grade whether it was sent.
- **The C1 draft goes to an AI for critique.** It is the group's own work; use a tool approved for coursework.
- **B3 and D1 are about structures.** Keep the discussion away from named colleagues or administrators.
- **D1 builds empathy both ways.** Students who see the workload behind a process-evidence rubric tend to write
  better process records.
- **Looking ahead:** the [Chapter 18 set](ch18-the-institutions-toolkit.md) uses the institution's forms, the
  classification worksheet and the disclosure template, from the student's side.
