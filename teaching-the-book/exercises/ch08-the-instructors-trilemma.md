# Exercises for Chapter 8: The Instructor's Trilemma

*Part II, the instructor guide, read from the student's side of the desk. Section numbers refer to v6.1.1 of the
book. See [how the exercises are built](../exercise-design.md).*

*Each exercise starts with its assignment category from Section 3.4: NAI (No AI), AIT (AI as Tutor), AIC (AI as Collaborator), or AIS (AI as Object of Study). If your instructor sets a different category, follow theirs.*

## Key points

| | Key point | Section | Exercises |
|---|---|---|---|
| K1 | A course now has to assess two things separately: what you can do on your own, and what you can do responsibly with AI. The instructor guide is about how to engage with AI, not whether. | 8.1 | A1, B1 |
| K2 | Polished work no longer shows who understood it. The question an instructor now asks of every submission is what it shows that you can understand, explain, verify, and do. | 8.3 | A1, B1, C1 |
| K3 | An assignment can serve four purposes: measure unaided knowledge, teach responsible tool use, produce a polished product, or reveal process. Trouble starts when one task is asked to do all four. | 8.3 | B1, E1 |
| K4 | With AI, the work can improve while the learning does not. Students skip the self-checking and the diagnosis that ordinary help-seeking includes. | 8.3 | A4, C1 |
| K5 | Office hours were an early warning, and a question taken to a chatbot removes it. Reliance on AI can look like self-sufficiency. The help a person offers that AI does not fill on its own is to be the audience: to listen and report what did not land. | 8.3 | B2, C2 |
| K6 | The Instructor's Trilemma: professional sustainability, trustworthy judgment, and student learning. Any two can be kept at the cost of the third: hollow understanding, quiet overclaim, or the integrity penalty. | 8.4, 8.4.1 | A2, B3, E2 |
| K7 | How students feel about their learning is not a measure of it. In one physics trial, students in active sections learned more and rated their learning lower. | 8.4.2 | A4, D1 |
| K8 | Some controls work only while the tools are weak. What lasts is what you can show in person: an explanation in your own words, a reconstruction, a transfer to a new case. A spot check decides whose work gets a closer look, not who is punished. | 8.4.4 | A3, D2 |
| K9 | Three design axes shape a course: class size decides how you are assessed, level decides what, and discipline decides what checking looks like. | 8.5 | B3 |

## A. From memory (about 10 minutes)

Answer without notes. Then check each answer in the section the key-points table gives for it, and mark what you missed.

**A1.** *NAI.* State in your own words the question Section 8.3 says an instructor now asks of every piece of
submitted work. Why can't a polished final product answer it any more?

**A2.** *NAI.* Name the three corners of the Instructor's Trilemma. Name the failure on each edge, and say which two
corners each one keeps.

**A3.** *NAI.* Section 8.4.4 sorts controls into two kinds. Give one example of each. What does a spot check
decide, and what does it not decide?

**A4.** *NAI.* What did Fan and colleagues find about essay scores and knowledge gain? What did Deslauriers and
colleagues find about active learning and how students rated it?

## B. In your own courses

**B1. What does this assignment show?** *NAI. About 20 minutes.* Pick two assignments you have this term. For each,
answer the question from Section 8.3: what would a strong submission show that you can understand, explain,
verify, and do? Could a generic AI tool complete it well? Then say which of the four purposes it serves (unaided
knowledge, responsible tool use, polished product, or process) and whether the course says so.
*Submit:* a short table, one row per assignment.
*Graded on:* whether each answer names something specific you would have to do, not a topic.

**B2. The warning you didn't send.** *NAI. About 15 minutes.* Think of the last time you took a question to a
chatbot instead of a person: your instructor, a TA, or a classmate. In half a page, say what the person would have
learned about where you were, and what the chatbot could not tell you about yourself. Then name one way your
instructor offers to be your audience (Section 8.3), and when you could use it this month. Leave out anything
personal.
*Submit:* the half page.
*Graded on:* whether you name a specific question and a specific chance to ask it.

**B3. Which corner is your course protecting?** *NAI. About 15 minutes.* Read the syllabus and grading scheme of
one course. Which choices protect trustworthy judgment (in-person exams, oral checks, process records)? Which
protect learning (practice, feedback, revision)? Which keep the workload manageable? Name the failure from Section
8.4.1 the course would drift toward if one corner gave way, and how the course's size and level (Section 8.5) shape
its choices. Judge the design only. Do not guess at your instructor's job or workload.
*Submit:* one paragraph.
*Graded on:* whether each claim points to something in the syllabus.

## C. With AI

**C1. Polished, but whose?** *AIS, then NAI for the translations. About 45 minutes.*

1. Pick a problem from a course you are taking, of a kind you have practiced. Use one the course allows AI on
   for practice, or one from a past term.
2. Ask an AI for a full solution. Save it.
3. Without AI or notes, apply the translation test from Section 8.3 to the AI's solution. Re-present its key step
   in two other forms: a picture or diagram, and a plain-language explanation with a new example.
4. Solve a similar problem yourself, without AI. Apply the same test to your own solution.
5. Compare. Which key step could you translate? Where did you get stuck? What does the AI's polished solution show
   about what you can do?

*Submit:* the AI's solution, your four translations, your own solution, and half a page answering step 5. Add a
one-line disclosure.
*Graded on:* the quality of the translations, and how honestly you report where you got stuck.

**C2. Ask for an audience, not a rewrite.** *AIT. About 30 minutes.*

1. Take an explanation you have written: a solution, a lab discussion, or a paragraph of an argument. Use work your
   course allows AI on.
2. Ask an AI to act as your audience only: to say where it lost the thread or where a step was not justified, and
   not to rewrite or fix anything. If it rewrites, ask again.
3. Explain the same work aloud to a classmate who agrees to do the same, without notes.
4. List what each caught. Which caught the step you least understood?

*Submit:* the AI's comments (or a shared link), your notes from the classmate, the list, and a one-line disclosure.
*Graded on:* whether the list separates what each audience caught, and whether you held the AI to listening.

## D. Going further ★

**D1. Feeling versus learning.** *NAI. Two weeks.* After each study session for one course, rate how much you
learned, from 1 to 5, and note how you studied (rereading, practice problems, explaining aloud, AI help). A week
later, test yourself on the same material without notes. In one page, compare your ratings with your results.
Which ways of studying felt best? Which left the most behind? Set your findings beside the physics trial in Section
8.4.2.

**D2. What you would rather be checked on.** *NAI. One page.* Section 8.4.4 says the controls that last are the
ones that test what you can show in person. Compare two designs you could meet in a course: one built on a rule
against using AI for homework, and one built on short oral checks of homework chosen at random. From your side of
the desk, which tells your instructor more about you? Which would you learn more from, and which would you rather
face? Use the line that a spot check decides whose work gets a closer look, not who is punished.

## E. One week later (about 10 minutes)

**E1.** *NAI.* Without notes, state the question from Section 8.3 and the four purposes an assignment can serve.
Which purpose does your next assignment serve?

**E2.** *NAI.* Without notes, name the three corners of the Instructor's Trilemma and the three failures. Which
failure would cost you most as a student, and why?

## What a strong answer looks like: C1

Two condensed responses to C1 from students in an introductory statistics course. The AI was asked to interpret a
95% confidence interval for mean commute time of 22 to 28 minutes. Only one student did the exercise.

**A weak response**

> *AI solution:* "We are 95% confident that the true mean commute time is between 22 and 28 minutes."
>
> *Translations:* Picture: a number line from 22 to 28 with the mean in the middle. Plain language: we are pretty
> sure the average commute is between 22 and 28 minutes.
>
> *Own solution and translations:* similar.
>
> *Half page:* The AI's answer was clear and correct, and I could restate it, so I understand confidence intervals.
>
> *Disclosure:* Used AI.

**A strong response**

> *AI solution:* "We are 95% confident that the true mean commute time is between 22 and 28 minutes. If we took
> many samples and built an interval from each, about 95% of those intervals would contain the true mean."
>
> *Translations:* Picture: I started to draw a bell curve over 22 to 28 with 95% of the area shaded, and stopped.
> The shading said the true mean has a 95% chance of being in this interval, which is not what the second sentence
> says. I redrew it as many intervals from repeated samples stacked above a fixed true mean, most of them crossing
> it. Plain language, new example: if a factory's process makes good intervals 95% of the time, this interval came
> from that process; it either contains the true mean or it doesn't.
>
> *Own solution, a 90% interval for weekly study hours:* I wrote the interpretation, then drew the stacked
> intervals without stopping.
>
> *Half page:* I could repeat the AI's first sentence, but I could not draw it until I fixed my picture. The
> polished answer showed what a good interpretation looks like. It did not show that I knew what the 95% refers to;
> my first picture showed I didn't. My own solution translated only because the AI's version had made me fix the
> picture first.
>
> *Disclosure:* AI wrote the solution in step 2 (shared link above); the translations and my own solution were done
> without AI.

**What separates them**

- The weak translations restate the AI's sentence in other words. The strong ones change form, and the change
  exposes a misreading.
- The weak half page treats being able to repeat an answer as understanding it. The strong one says what the AI's
  answer shows and what it doesn't, which is the question Section 8.3 asks.
- The weak disclosure doesn't say where AI was used. The strong one does.

## For instructors

- **Minimum week:** A1 to A4, B1, and C1, then E1 a week later. About ninety minutes of student time.
- **C1 is the anchor.** It puts the calibration problem of Section 8.3 in the student's hands. The translations are
  the graded product, so a student who used the AI's words gets little credit.
- **Read B2 with care.** Students may say they avoid office hours. That is information about the course's signals,
  not a confession. Section 8.3 suggests building the signal into the course instead.
- **B3 asks students to judge design, not people.** Do not ask students to reason about any instructor's
  employment status. Section 8.4.2 is about working conditions in general.
- **D1 needs a delayed self-test.** Without it, the ratings are only feelings. It pairs with C1 in the
  [Chapter 6 set](ch06-judgment-and-limits.md).
- **Privacy:** no exercise needs graded work, a classmate's work, or personal data uploaded. C2 uses the student's
  own writing on work the course allows AI on.
- **Looking ahead:** Chapter 9 turns the four purposes into the four assignment categories, and Chapter 10 sets out
  the assessment models that make the core question answerable at each class size.
