# Exercises for Chapter 13: The Instructor's Toolkit

*Part II, the instructor guide, read from the student's side of the desk. Section numbers refer to v6.1.1 of the
book. See [how the exercises are built](../exercise-design.md). Chapter 13 collects tools, so this set is short:
it asks you to use the tools that are aimed at you and to read the others as the person they are used on.*

*Each exercise starts with its assignment category from Section 3.4: NAI (No AI), AIT (AI as Tutor), AIC (AI as Collaborator), or AIS (AI as Object of Study). If your instructor sets a different category, follow theirs.*

## Key points

| | Key point | Section | Exercises |
|---|---|---|---|
| K1 | The syllabus statements say, for each category, what you may and may not do. Under AIT, no AI-generated text, code, or solutions enter what you submit. Under AIC, you are responsible for every claim, source, calculation, and line of code. | 13.1.1 | A1, B1 |
| K2 | A filled assignment label settles AI use before the work begins: allowed and disallowed uses, disclosure, verification, the independent component, an optional help ladder, privacy, and grading. | 13.1.2 | B1 |
| K3 | The disclosure block makes honesty cheap. Six fields: tools used, tool role, your role, what you accepted, changed, or rejected, how you verified, and what you can explain without AI. | 13.2.1 | A2, C1 |
| K4 | When a submission looks wrong, an instructor is asked to pause, reread the assignment, separate what you can do from whether you acted in bad faith, gather process evidence, talk with you first, and escalate by seriousness, not certainty. | 13.3 | A3 |
| K5 | The documentation for AI-integrated work: tools and versions, a plan written before AI, the outputs that influenced the work, your verification, and your final reasoning in your own words. It should stay short. | 13.4.3 | C1 |
| K6 | The oral defense rubric has six criteria, and the integrity row is never part of the mark. The audit script asks six questions, the last a transfer question without AI. | 13.5 | E1 |
| K7 | Of the four safeguards, a minimum Core Competence threshold to pass matters most: fluent AI use cannot make up for weak foundations. | 13.6 | A4 |
| K8 | The AI critique template: say what the response does well, find at least two weaknesses, repair one part, and say what the example teaches about AI in the discipline. | 13.9 | C2 |

## A. From memory (about 10 minutes)

Answer without notes. Then check each answer in the section the key-points table gives for it, and mark what you missed.

**A1.** *NAI.* Under the AI-as-tutor statement in Section 13.1.1, what may you use AI for, and what may not appear
in your submission?

**A2.** *NAI.* Name the six fields of the student disclosure block.

**A3.** *NAI.* List the six steps an instructor is asked to follow when a submission looks wrong. Which question is
answered first, and why?

**A4.** *NAI.* Name the four safeguards in Section 13.6. Which matters most, and why?

## B. In your own courses

**B1. Fill in the label.** *NAI. About 20 minutes.* Take one assignment you have this term. Fill in the assignment
label template from Section 13.1.2 as far as the assignment's own wording allows. Leave a field blank where the
wording does not say. For each blank field, write the question you would ask.
*Submit:* the filled label and your questions.
*Graded on:* whether each entry quotes or points to the assignment, and whether the questions are specific.

## C. With AI

**C1. A full disclosure.** *AIT, or AIC if your course sets it. About 30 minutes, on top of the assignment.* On your
next assignment that allows AI, keep the documentation in Section 13.4.3 as you work: tools and versions, a plan
written before any AI use, only the outputs that changed your work, your check, and your final reasoning. Then
fill in the six-field disclosure block. Keep the whole record to one page.
*Submit:* the record and the block, with the assignment if your instructor wants this attached to it.
*Graded on:* whether each field reports something specific, and whether the record is short.

**C2. Critique an AI answer.** *AIS. About 40 minutes.* Ask an AI to answer a question from your course, one you
can check. Then follow the template in Section 13.9: say what it does well, find at least two weaknesses or
unsupported claims, repair one part, and say in two sentences what the example teaches about AI in your field.
Check your critique against a source you trust, not against another AI.
*Submit:* the AI's answer, your four parts, the source you checked against, and a one-line disclosure.
*Graded on:* whether the weaknesses are real and the repair holds.

## D. Going further ★

**D1. Pitfalls from the other side.** *NAI. One page.* Section 13.7 lists six instructor pitfalls. Choose two. For
each, describe what the pitfall would feel like to a student on the receiving end, and what a student could do
about it: ask a question, keep process evidence, or use the fair response sequence in Section 11.2.2. Write about
courses in general, not about a person.

## E. One week later (about 10 minutes)

**E1.** *NAI.* Without notes, write the six questions of the random audit script. Answer them for the last
assignment you submitted.

## What a strong answer looks like: C1

Two condensed disclosure blocks for a statistics assignment, under AIC, that asked for an analysis of whether a
tutoring program raised test scores.

**A weak response**

> *Tools used:* ChatGPT.
> *Tool role:* Helped with the analysis.
> *Student role:* Wrote the report.
> *Accepted, changed, or rejected:* Accepted most of it.
> *Independent verification:* Checked that the numbers looked right.
> *What I can explain without AI:* All of it.

**A strong response**

> *Tools used:* ChatGPT (the version named in the app, recorded on the date of use) and R.
> *Tool role:* Suggested a two-sample t-test and wrote the R code for the summary table.
> *Student role:* Wrote the plan before using AI, chose the test, ran and checked the code, and wrote the report.
> *Accepted, changed, or rejected:* Rejected the two-sample test: the same students were tested before and after,
> so I used a paired test. Kept the table code after checking it against two rows by hand.
> *Independent verification:* Recomputed two group means by hand; confirmed the pairing from the data dictionary;
> checked the test's assumptions with a plot of the differences.
> *What I can explain without AI:* Why a paired test fits, what the confidence interval for the mean difference
> says, and why the result does not show the program caused the gain, since there was no comparison group.

**What separates them**

- The weak block names a tool and stops. The strong block says what the tool did and what the student did.
- The strong block records a rejection, and the reason for it. That line answers one of the questions in
  Section 9.1.3 that make judgment visible: what did you accept, reject, or revise?
- "Looked right" is not a check. The strong block names three checks that do not depend on the AI.
- "All of it" invites an audit question the student may not answer. The strong block names what the student could
  defend, including a limit of the study.

## For instructors

- **Minimum week:** A1 to A4, B1, and C1, then E1 a week later. About ninety minutes of student time, most of it
  inside a real assignment.
- **C1 is the anchor.** It is the disclosure block in use. Grade it on specificity, and attach it to a real
  assignment if you can, so the record does work the student needed to do anyway.
- **B1 often finds blank fields.** That is information for you, not a failure by the student (Section 11.2.1: a
  first case is usually better evidence about the assignment than about the student).
- **D1 is about design in general.** Keep the discussion away from any particular instructor.
- **Privacy:** C2 uses an AI answer, not student work. C1 stays inside the student's own submission.
- **Looking ahead:** Part III's sets ask what a student's university protects and leaves out. The disclosure block
  returns in the institution's toolkit (Section 18.3.3).
