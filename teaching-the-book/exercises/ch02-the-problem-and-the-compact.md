# Exercises for Chapter 2: The Problem and the Compact

*Part I, the student guide. Section numbers refer to v6.1.1 of the book. See [how the exercises are built](../exercise-design.md).*

*Each exercise starts with its assignment category from Section 3.4: NAI (No AI), AIT (AI as Tutor), AIC (AI as Collaborator), or AIS (AI as Object of Study). If your instructor sets a different category, follow theirs.*

## Key points

| | Key point | Section | Exercises |
|---|---|---|---|
| K1 | The Student's Trilemma is a three-way pull among earning the grade, being honest about how the work happened, and showing that you understand. When the work is within your reach, all three hold. In the gap, you can hold any two, and the third gives way. | 2.1 | A1, D2 |
| K2 | Each corner that gives way has a name: hollow understanding, quiet overclaim, and the integrity penalty. Use them as early warnings, and look for the smallest change that restores the corner. The integrity penalty usually sits in the assignment or the grading, so surface it rather than carry the cost alone. | 2.1.1 | A1, A2, B2, C1, E2 |
| K3 | The trilemma pulls differently on different students, and it is hard to see from outside. Recognizing the pull you are under makes what you do next a choice rather than a reflex. | 2.1.2, 2.1.3 | A2, D2 |
| K4 | Some tasks are a road worth walking: the walking is the competence the course exists to build. Others are errands. Walk the road yourself and let AI check it; let AI carry the errand. | 2.1.3 | B1 |
| K5 | Five findings explain durable learning: practice testing, distributed practice, interleaving, the generation effect, and desirable difficulties. Easy is not the same as effective. | 2.2 | A3, C1, E1 |
| K6 | Calibration error is the distance between what you think you know and what you can demonstrate. AI widens it in five places: the recognition-production gap, the mastery illusion on transfer, and the effort, authorship, and tool-use illusions. | 2.2.1 | A3, C1, C2, D1 |
| K7 | Five calibration checks run in the moment: predict before reading, the forced unassisted gap, blank-page rehearsal, a self-quiz on the next day, and explanation aloud. They are early warnings, not verdicts. | 2.2.2 | C1, D1, E1 |
| K8 | The compact has five parts: Attempt, Use, Verify, Disclose, Own. Own means you can explain the work without saying "The AI told me." The translation test checks this: can you say the same thing visually, mathematically, and in plain language? | 2.3 | A4, C1, C2, E2 |

## A. From memory (about 10 minutes)

Answer without notes. Then check each answer in the section the key-points table gives for it, and mark what you missed.

**A1.** *NAI.* Name the three corners of the Student's Trilemma. For each pair you can hold, name the corner that
gives way and the failure mode that results. When is there no pull at all?

**A2.** *NAI.* Quiet overclaim can look harmless, since the student did learn the material. What does the chapter say
is lost? Why does a first-generation student feel this edge most sharply?

**A3.** *NAI.* Section 2.2 describes a study that compared rereading with testing. Which won when the test came five
minutes later, which won days later, and after which were students more confident? What does that tell you
about your own sense of progress?

**A4.** *NAI.* Name the five parts of the compact, in order. What does Own require you to be able to do? In the
translation test, what is the difference between a transcription and a translation?

## B. In your own courses

**B1. Road or errand.** *NAI. About 20 minutes.* A student in Section 2.1.3 asks: *If I am being asked to go from
point A to point B, why wouldn't I use a car to get there?* The chapter answers that for many tasks "the road
is the destination." Choose one assignment in a course you are taking. List the four to eight tasks it
involves. Mark each one road or errand, with one sentence on why. For one road task, say how AI could check
your work without carrying it. For one errand, say what you would check after AI carried it.
*Submit:* the list with labels and reasons, and your two sentences.
*Graded on:* whether each label rests on what the course exists to build, not on how tedious the task feels.

**B2. Three warning signs.** *NAI. About 15 minutes.* Choose an assignment due in the next two weeks. Section 2.1.1
gives one sign for sliding toward each failure mode. For each mode, write what that sign would look like on
this assignment, in one concrete sentence. Then write the smallest change you would make if you saw it: one
that restores the corner without letting the other two slip. For the integrity penalty, write the question you
would ask, and whom you would ask. This exercise is about what could happen. You are not asked to report on
past work.
*Submit:* three signs and three changes.
*Graded on:* whether each sign is specific enough to notice while you work, and whether each change protects
all three corners.

## C. With AI

**C1. Run the compact once.** *AIT. About 60 minutes.*

Use a practice problem from a course you are taking: an unassigned textbook exercise or an old practice
problem, not work you will submit for a grade. Use AI only if your course allows it for practice.

1. **Attempt.** Without AI, work the problem until you find where you are actually stuck. Write one sentence
   that names the spot.
2. **Use.** Ask an AI for help that keeps you active: a hint or a question, not the solution. Use your
   configuration from Chapter 1 (exercise B1) and the Study Partner Protocol
   ([`companion/spp/`](../../companion/spp/)).
3. **Predict before reading.** Before each new step in the AI's replies, write the step you expect. Record at
   least three predictions and whether each one matched.
4. **Rate yourself.** When the problem feels done, and before you close the AI, rate from 1 to 5 how well you
   could now do it alone. Write one sentence on why.
5. **Blank-page rehearsal.** Close the AI and your notes. Rebuild the key step on a blank page. Mark where you
   stalled.
6. **Translation test.** Still without AI, put the key idea in two forms other than the one you worked in: a
   picture, notation, or plain language.
7. **Verify.** Check one claim from the AI's replies against your textbook, notes, or instructor. Record what
   you found.

Then answer, in half a page:

- Compare your rating in step 4 with your rebuild in step 5. How large was your calibration error? Which of the
  five illusions in Section 2.2.1 best explains it?
- Section 2.2.2 calls these checks early warnings, not verdicts. What would be a verdict for this material in
  your course?
- If this had been graded work, which corner of the Student's Trilemma would have given way? What is the
  smallest change that would protect it?

*Submit:* your stuck sentence, your predictions, your rating, your rebuild, your two translations, the claim
you checked and how, a shared link to the conversation (or a short excerpt), and your half page. Add a one-line
disclosure.
*Graded on:* the honesty of the comparison between your rating and your rebuild, and the claim you checked. A
rebuild that stalls, reported accurately, can earn full marks. The quality of the AI's replies does not count.

**C2. Transcription or translation?** *AIS. About 30 minutes.*

The figure in Section 2.3 separates two things. A transcription reads the symbols aloud, and anyone can produce
it. A translation says what the statement asserts "in words that never lean on the symbols." This exercise asks
whether an AI's version is one or the other.

1. Choose a short formal statement from a course you are taking: a definition, theorem, law, or rule. In a
   field with little notation, choose a dense sentence from a reading.
2. Without AI, write your own plain-language translation.
3. Ask a fresh AI for three versions: one in plain language, one as a picture or diagram described in words,
   and one in new notation. For a reading, ask for the same idea with a different example in place of new
   notation.
4. Label each reply transcription or translation, with one reason. For a reading, treat a paraphrase that only
   swaps words for synonyms as a transcription.
5. Check one reply against your course materials. Does it keep the meaning, or change it?

Then answer, in a short paragraph each:

- Which reply looked like a translation but was not? What gave it away?
- The AI moved between forms with ease. Why does that tell you nothing about whether you own the idea?
- Did reading the AI's versions make you feel you understood more than your own translation showed? If so,
  name the illusion from Section 2.2.1.

*Submit:* the statement, your translation, the three replies (a shared link or excerpts), your labels with
reasons, your check, and your answers. Add a one-line disclosure.
*Graded on:* whether each label is argued from what the statement means, and whether your own translation keeps
the meaning without the symbols. Whether the AI did well or badly does not count.

## D. Going further ★

**D1. Two weeks of unassisted gaps.** *AIT. Two weeks.* For two weeks of practice work where your course
allows AI, run the forced unassisted gap from Section 2.2.2. Each time AI helps you with a problem, close it and
try the next problem of the same type alone. Before you start that problem, predict how it will go: smooth, a
few stalls, or stuck. Then record what happened. Log at least six pairs, and run a self-quiz on the next day for
at least three of them. Report in one page: did your predictions get closer to what happened? Which illusion
from Section 2.2.1 showed up most? What, if anything, did you change in how you use AI? Add a one-line
disclosure.

**D2. Hard to see.** *NAI. One page.* Section 2.1.3 says the trilemma is invisible to the people best placed
to remove it. Choose one of the four pressures in Section 2.1.2 and a course you know well. Explain how a
student under that pressure would feel the pull in that course, and why the instructor would not see it. Then
propose one change to the assignment, the grading, or the disclosure rule that would ease the pull. Use the
table in Section 2.1.4 to say which of the instructor's three corners your change would strain, and how anyone
would know it worked. Write about the pressure in general terms. You do not need to say whether it applies to
you.

## E. One week later (5 minutes)

**E1.** *NAI.* Without notes, rebuild the key step from C1 on a blank page. Compare it with last week's rebuild and
with your rating in step 4. What stayed, and what went?

**E2.** *NAI.* From memory, write the five parts of the compact and the three failure modes. Then write your
translation from C2 again, and compare it with the one you wrote a week ago.

## What a strong answer looks like: C1

Two condensed responses to C1 from a student in first-semester calculus. The practice problem: a 5-meter ladder
leans against a wall, and its foot slides out at 0.5 meters per second. How fast is the top sliding down when
the foot is 3 meters from the wall? Both used the same AI setup. Only one did the exercise.

**A weak response**

> *Stuck:* I didn't know how to start.
>
> *Predictions:* 1. Use the Pythagorean theorem. Matched. 2. Take the derivative. Matched. 3. Get the answer.
> Matched.
>
> *Rating:* 5. The AI explained it clearly.
>
> *Rebuild:* x² + y² = 25, differentiate, plug in. The top falls at 0.375 m/s. No problems.
>
> *Translations:* Plain language: "x squared plus y squared equals 25, so 2x dx/dt plus 2y dy/dt equals 0."
> Picture: a right triangle.
>
> *Claim checked:* The answer matched the back of the book.
>
> *Half page (condensed):* My calibration error was small, because I rated 5 and rebuilt it. No illusion
> applied. The verdict would be the test. If this had been graded, no corner would have given way, because I
> understood it.

**A strong response**

> *Stuck:* I wrote x² + y² = 25 and put in x = 3 right away. Then I had nothing left to differentiate.
>
> *Predictions:* 1. I expected "use Pythagoras." The AI asked instead what stays fixed and what changes.
> Missed. 2. I expected 2x + 2y = 0. It wrote 2x (dx/dt) + 2y (dy/dt) = 0. Missed: I forgot that both
> lengths change with time. 3. I expected the top's rate to be positive. It came out negative, because the
> top falls. Missed.
>
> *Rating:* 4. Every step made sense once I saw it.
>
> *Rebuild:* I set up the equation and stalled at the derivative. I wrote 2x + 2y = 0 again, the same mistake.
> I fixed it only after asking myself what changes with time. The top falls at 0.375 m/s.
>
> *Translations:* Picture: the ladder as the long side of a right triangle, fixed at 5, with an arrow pushing
> the foot out and an arrow pulling the top down. The top's arrow starts short and grows as the ladder
> flattens. Plain language: the ladder cannot stretch, so as the foot slides out, the top must come down. Near
> the wall the top barely moves. As the ladder gets flatter, the same push at the foot makes the top drop
> faster.
>
> *Claim checked:* The AI said, "Never plug in numbers before you differentiate." Our textbook's related-rates
> section warns only against putting in quantities that change with time. The ladder's 5 meters never
> changes, so it can go in early. The AI's rule was too broad.
>
> *Half page (condensed):* I rated myself 4 and then stalled at the same step that stopped me at the start.
> That gap is my calibration error. It fits the recognition-production gap: I recognized the chain rule step
> when the AI wrote it, but I could not produce it. The blank page is only a warning. The verdict is the
> closed-book quiz in two weeks. If this had been graded homework, I would have handed in a right answer with
> an honest disclosure, and still missed that step on the quiz. That is hollow understanding. The smallest
> change is two more related-rates problems from a blank page before the quiz.

**What separates them**

- The weak stuck sentence and predictions commit to nothing, so nothing can be checked. The strong ones commit,
  and three missed predictions show exactly where the student's picture of the problem was wrong.
- The weak rating and rebuild agree because nothing was tested. The strong rebuild stalls at the same step as
  the attempt. That stall, beside a rating of 4, is the calibration error the exercise exists to find.
- The weak translations read the symbols aloud. That is transcription, which can be produced without
  understanding. The strong ones drop the symbols, and the picture shows something the equation hides: the top
  speeds up as the ladder flattens.
- The weak check confirms a number. The strong one tests a rule the AI stated, against a named source, and
  finds it too broad.
- The strong response reports the worse rebuild and earns the higher grade. C1 grades the account, not the
  performance.

## For instructors

- **Minimum week:** A1 to A4, B2, and C1, then E1 a week later. About ninety minutes of student time.
- **C1 is the anchor.** It runs all five parts of the compact on one practice problem. Its graded product is the
  comparison between the student's rating and the blank-page rebuild. An AI can write the half page but cannot
  do the rebuild with the AI closed.
- **Grade the account, not the rebuild.** If a stalled rebuild costs points, C1 puts students in the trilemma
  it teaches: an honest report would cost the grade, which is the integrity penalty. Section 2.1 argues that a
  well-designed course dissolves the trilemma. Let this exercise be a small example, and say so in class.
- **Expect some clean rebuilds.** Ask whether the rebuild used the student's own words or the AI's phrasing.
  Section 2.2.2 treats reaching for the AI's exact phrasing as a sign of the recognition-production gap.
- **Privacy:** no exercise needs anything private uploaded. C1 and C2 use practice problems and statements from
  course materials, not work the student will submit for a grade. A2 and D2 treat the pressures in Section
  2.1.2 in general terms. Do not ask students which pressures apply to them; some, such as visa status and
  family finances, are private. B2 asks about an upcoming assignment, not past conduct, so no one is asked to
  report a quiet overclaim.
- **The compact:** a printable one-page copy is in [`companion/compact/`](../../companion/compact/). Section 2.3
  notes that some courses adopt it as a signed agreement. If yours does, hand it out after C1, so students sign
  something they have already run once.
- **Looking ahead:** attempting first (Chapter 1, K3) is now the compact's first part. Section 2.1.4 previews
  how the rest of Part I answers the trilemma: the Learning Spiral (Section 3.2) lowers the integrity penalty,
  the translation test catches hollow understanding, and the disclosure templates (Section 4.5,
  [`students/disclosure-templates.md`](../../students/disclosure-templates.md)) ease the pull toward quiet
  overclaim. Section 2.2.2 already places each calibration check at a step of the Spiral, so the C1 log is a
  natural starting point for Chapter 3. The table in Section 2.1.4 is the bridge to Chapters 8 and 14, the
  Instructor's and the Institution's Trilemmas.
