# Exercises for Chapter 12: Implementation and Limits

*Part II, the instructor guide, read from the student's side of the desk. Section numbers refer to v6.1.1 of the
book. See [how the exercises are built](../exercise-design.md). Chapter 12 is short, and so is this set.*

*Each exercise starts with its assignment category from Section 3.4: NAI (No AI), AIT (AI as Tutor), AIC (AI as Collaborator), or AIS (AI as Object of Study). If your instructor sets a different category, follow theirs.*

## Key points

| | Key point | Section | Exercises |
|---|---|---|---|
| K1 | Graders calibrate by scoring the same samples independently, comparing, and clarifying the rubric. The goal is a shared reading of the course's standards. | 12.1 | A1, B1 |
| K2 | The three smallest moves that change a course: a category on every task, one independent check per unit, and a brief disclosure on any AIC assignment. A course can get the rest across more than one term. | 12.2 | A2, B2 |
| K3 | Students follow a policy more readily when they know its purpose. Students who argued sample cases and drafted the rules produced policies that drew more support and covered cases an instructor alone might miss. The instructor still owns the final wording. | 12.2 | C1 |
| K4 | The evidence on AI tutoring is mixed, and effects vary by discipline, duration, and assessment. The design principle: allow AI where it advances the learning goal and constrain it where it hides the goal. The standard: judge a technology by whose learning it deepens. | 12.3 | A3, D1 |

## A. From memory (about 10 minutes)

Answer without notes. Then check each answer in the section the key-points table gives for it, and mark what you missed.

**A1.** *NAI.* Describe the calibration process in Section 12.1. What is its goal?

**A2.** *NAI.* Name the three smallest moves from Section 12.2. Which of the seven steps does the chapter single
out, and why?

**A3.** *NAI.* State the design principle and the standard at the end of Section 12.3.

## B. In your own courses

**B1. Calibrate with classmates.** *NAI. About 30 minutes, in groups of three.* Take the weak and strong sample
answers from two exercise sets in this folder, with their "What separates them" notes covered. Each of you scores
all four answers alone on the four-level rubric in Section 11.5.1. Compare your scores. Where you disagreed, find
the words in the rubric that let you read it differently, and write one sentence that would settle it.
*Submit:* your three sets of scores and the clarifying sentence.
*Graded on:* whether the sentence would resolve the disagreement you actually had.

**B2. The three moves, from your seat.** *NAI. About 15 minutes.* For one course, check the three moves in Section
12.2. Does every task carry a category? Is there an independent check in each unit? Does AI-assisted work ask for a
disclosure? For any move that is missing, say what you would do differently as a student if it were there.
*Submit:* a three-row table and two sentences.
*Graded on:* whether each row points to the syllabus or an assignment.

## C. With AI

**C1. Rules from cases.** *NAI, then AIT for the critique. About 60 minutes, in groups of four.*

This is the exercise Section 12.2 proposes, run by students for practice. It drafts no rule for a real course.

1. Take three cases: a weekly coding exercise, a reading response, and a take-home problem.
2. Argue each case. Which uses of AI should be allowed, and which should not? Draft a rule of two or three
   sentences for each, naming its category from Section 9.2.
3. Ask an AI to act as a critic: list situations each rule does not settle, without rewriting the rule. If it
   rewrites, ask again.
4. As a group, decide which gaps matter and revise the rules yourselves.

*Submit:* the first drafts, the AI's list (or a shared link), the revised rules, and a one-line disclosure.
*Graded on:* whether each rule names a category and a check a student could actually run, and how the group
handled the gaps.

## D. Going further ★

**D1. Whose learning does it deepen?** *NAI. One page.* Pick one AI tool you or your classmates use for coursework.
Apply the standard in Section 12.3. Who in your class is likely to learn more with it, and who less? What evidence
would tell you? The trials summarized in Section 6.4 are a place to start.

## E. One week later (about 10 minutes)

**E1.** *NAI.* Without notes, name the three smallest moves and state the standard in Section 12.3. Which move would
change your own work most in the course you know best?

## What a strong answer looks like: C1

Two condensed group responses to C1, for the weekly coding exercise only.

**A weak response**

> *Rule:* AI may be used responsibly on coding exercises.
>
> *AI critique:* (not requested)
>
> *Revised rule:* AI may be used responsibly on coding exercises, with disclosure.

**A strong response**

> *First draft (AIT):* You may ask AI to explain an error message or a library function, and to critique code you
> wrote. You may not submit code an AI wrote. Before asking, write one test you expect your code to fail.
>
> *AI critique, summarized:* The rule does not settle inline autocomplete in the editor, AI-written test cases, or
> code an AI rewrote line by line from your own.
>
> *Revised rule (AIT):* You may ask AI to explain an error message or a library function, and to critique code you
> wrote. You may not submit code an AI wrote or rewrote, including editor autocomplete: turn it off for these
> exercises. Tests count as code. Before asking, write one test you expect to fail, and add one line saying what
> the AI explained.
>
> *Disclosure:* An AI listed gaps in our draft (link above); the rules and the revisions are ours.

**What separates them**

- The weak rule names no category and no check, so it settles nothing (Section 9.2).
- The strong group asked the AI only for gaps, then decided for itself which mattered.
- The strong revision closes a real gap, autocomplete, that most students would meet in their first session.

## For instructors

- **Minimum week:** A1 to A3, B2, and C1, then E1 a week later. About ninety minutes of student time.
- **C1 is the anchor**, and it is practice. Section 12.2 reports that students who helped draft a course's AI
  policy supported it more. If you want to run that for real, C1 is the rehearsal; you still own the final wording.
- **B1 uses only the public sample answers** in this folder, so no student work is shared. C1 sends an AI only
  the rules the group drafted for the exercise.
- **Looking ahead:** the [Chapter 13 set](ch13-the-instructors-toolkit.md) reads the toolkit's syllabus language,
  disclosure block, and audit script from the student's side.
