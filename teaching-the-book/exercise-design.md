# How the exercises are built

*Companion to [Teaching the book](README.md).*

An end-of-chapter exercise is worth assigning only if doing it changes what the student can do a week later.
That standard comes from the learning research the book relies on. Practice testing and spaced practice are the
best-supported study techniques; self-explanation and interleaving also help (Dunlosky et al., 2013). Producing
an answer teaches more than being shown one, and a test is a learning event, not only a measurement (Bjork and
Bjork, 2011). AI adds one more standard: an exercise that an AI can complete without the student learning
anything is broken.

## Ten tests every exercise meets

1. **It maps to a key point.** Each exercise targets one named idea from the chapter, and the set as a whole
   covers every key point.
2. **The student produces something first.** An answer, a prediction, or an explanation comes before the
   student consults the book or an AI.
3. **Difficulty rises through the set.** From checking understanding, to applying the idea, to carrying it to
   a new setting. Starred exercises are open-ended.
4. **Some questions use no AI at all.** Short questions answered from memory are the cheapest exercise that
   works.
5. **Ideas come back.** Later sets reuse earlier ideas, and every set ends with a check a week later. That check
   is the only part that measures learning rather than performance.
6. **It moves to the student's own field.** Exercises say "in a course you are taking" rather than fixing a
   subject, because the book's readers cross disciplines.
7. **It trains judgment of one's own learning.** Students predict, then check. The gap between the two is the
   lesson the book teaches: the feeling of progress is not learning.
8. **It holds up when AI is available.** The graded product is the student's judgment, explanation, or record
   of checking, or else the AI is the object being studied. Every exercise opens with its assignment category
   from Section 3.4: NAI (No AI), AIT (AI as Tutor), AIC (AI as Collaborator), or AIS (AI as Object of Study).
9. **It is clear and bounded.** A time estimate, a defined thing to submit, and a statement of what the grade
   rests on.
10. **The difficulty is the useful kind.** Hard enough to take effort, within reach of what the chapter has
    taught.

## The shape of each chapter's set

| Section | What it does | Category |
|---|---|---|
| **Key points** | Lists the chapter's main ideas and which exercises practice each | none |
| **A. From memory** | Three or four short questions answered without notes | NAI |
| **B. In your own courses** | Applies the chapter to a course the student is taking | NAI |
| **C. With AI** | Uses AI in a way that practices the chapter | AIT, AIC, or AIS |
| **D. Going further** ★ | Open-ended or longer work | Any of the four |
| **E. One week later** | A short check of what stuck | NAI |
| **What a strong answer looks like** | A weak and a strong response to the set's main exercise, with notes on what separates them | none |

There are no private answer keys. Most exercises have no single right answer, and the grade rests on reasoning,
so a public example of a strong response teaches the standard without letting anyone skip the work. The one
exercise with a true key is Chapter 4's C3, built on AI-style answers with planted errors. Its answers are
public too, so students can practice and check themselves. An instructor who grades C3 writes a fresh pair
each term, and the set's instructor notes say how.

The sample answers are illustrations written for each set. Their numbers, tallies, and tables are invented to
show the standard; they are not data from a real class.

## Parts II and III: the student's side of the desk

Parts II and III are written for instructors and for the people who run departments and universities. In a
course, the readers are students. Their exercises keep the student's point of view:

- **Part II (teaching and assessment):** how a course's AI rules, assignments, and assessments shape the
  student's own learning; which designs the student would learn most from, and why; what fair checking looks
  like from the receiving end.
- **Part III (departments and institutions):** what the student's own university or department protects and
  leaves out; how a policy reads to the person it governs; what one change the student would propose, and how
  anyone would know it worked.

Students are never asked to design a course or write a policy as if they held the job. They are asked to judge
what the book proposes, using its vocabulary, from where they sit.

## Disclosure and privacy

Any exercise that uses AI ends with a one-line disclosure (Section 4.5). No exercise asks a student to upload
anything private: their own work under review, a classmate's work, or personal data (Section 4.6). When an
exercise asks an AI to critique a draft, the draft is the one written for that exercise, never graded work.
